Task 8 puts a strange photograph on your screen and asks you to describe it to somebody who is not
looking at it. **Every word that points at the screen is wasted, because your listener cannot follow
your finger.** "This thing here" tells them nothing at all.

## The two failures

The first is pointing. You have the picture in front of you, so your language assumes the listener
does too: *this machine*, *over here on the right*, *as you can see*. It transmits nothing.

The second is more common. You say the scene is strange without saying **how**. "It's really unusual"
is a reaction, not a description — a listener hearing sixty seconds of reaction still cannot picture
one square inch of the thing. Oddness is built out of size, material, position and purpose, not
announced.

Notice what the sample answer below never does. **It contains no "this", no "here", and no "as you
can see" — not one.** Impose that constraint while you practise: removing those words forces the
other sentences to carry real information.

## The task

Task 8 is "Describing an Unusual Situation", the last of the eight Speaking tasks on Paragon's
test-format page. You get **30 seconds of preparation and 60 seconds of speaking** — about 130 to 160
words at a comfortable pace. Those per-task figures come from Paragon's Speaking study pack, not the
test-format page, which publishes the task names and the 15-minute component length but no clock for
each task. Sixty seconds is short enough that a slow start costs you a third of the answer.

## The prompt

> You took this photograph while walking through your neighbourhood. Describe what you see to a
> friend over the phone. Your friend cannot see the picture.
>
> *(original AlmiWorld practice prompt)*

The picture on the screen:

*A residential sidewalk in winter. On the strip of grass between the sidewalk and the road stands a
gumball machine — the old kind, a glass globe on a metal stand — except it is roughly as tall as an
adult. The globe is packed with yellow tennis balls instead of gum. The base is red-painted metal,
chipped along the edges, bolted to a concrete block. There is a coin slot and a chrome crank at waist
height, and a wide chute underneath with a rubber flap. Snow on the ground. A leash hook is screwed
into the pole.*

## Thirty seconds of preparation, honestly

You are not planning paragraphs in half a minute. You are making three decisions.

**One: what is it closest to?** Nothing in the picture has a name, so you borrow one. **The move that
carries this whole task is comparing an object that does not exist to one your listener already owns
in their head** — a gumball machine, a parking meter, a mailbox — then saying what is wrong with the
comparison. "It looks like a gumball machine, except it's the size of a person" does more work in
nine words than thirty seconds of adjectives.

**Two: which direction do you travel?** Top to bottom, because the object is vertical. Pick one
direction and do not double back.

**Three: what is your guess?** The leash hook is the clue. Decide before you speak, so the ending is
a landing and not a stop.

## The answer

> Okay, so — it looks like one of those gumball machines you'd see outside a grocery store, except
> it's the size of a person. That's the odd part. Maybe five feet tall, standing on a strip of grass
> beside a sidewalk, snow on the ground.
>
> Starting from the top: there's a big glass globe, round, completely full of tennis balls. Yellow
> ones. Not gum. Below the globe the base is red metal, painted, and the paint's chipped along the
> edges. At waist height there's a coin slot and a little chrome crank, the kind you turn. Underneath
> that, a wide chute with a rubber flap over it — wide enough for a tennis ball to roll out.
>
> Oh, and there's a leash hook bolted onto the pole. So my guess — and I could be wrong — is somebody
> on the street built it for dog walkers. You put in a quarter, you get a ball.

**About 150 words. Target: 60 seconds.**

No band or level is attached to that answer, and nobody honest would attach one — there is no
published weighting for the four dimensions, no answer key for Speaking, and no rater in the room.
What follows is not a mark. It is a reading.

## Read against the four dimensions

The four published Speaking dimensions are **Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability** and
**Task Fulfillment**.

**Content/Coherence.** The answer travels in one direction and never doubles back — globe, base,
crank, chute, then the hook as a deliberate afterthought. "Starting from the top" is the only
signpost it needs. A listener could sketch the object from the audio.

**Vocabulary.** The precise words are ordinary ones: *globe*, *chute*, *crank*, *rubber flap*,
*chipped*. Materials do the work — glass, red metal, chrome, rubber. Nothing is called "amazing",
which is a word about the speaker, not the object.

**Listenability.** Officially, *"How easy is it to listen to and understand the response?"*, with
*"Rhythm, pronunciation, and intonation"* among its sub-points. Short units help: "Yellow ones. Not
gum." is easier to follow at speed than a subordinate clause. Accent is not rated — Paragon's raters
*"specifically do not consider your accent"* — but being understood very much is.

**Task Fulfillment.** The task verb is *describe to someone who cannot see it*, and the answer treats
the listener as blind throughout. It names the oddness in sentence one and ends on an interpretation,
offered as a guess rather than a fact.

## What this answer is not

It is not a frame with the nouns swapped out. **This answer works because of its details, and its
details are the part you cannot borrow.** "The paint's chipped along the edges" is true of one object
in one photograph. So is "wide enough for a tennis ball to roll out". So is the leash hook — the only
reason the closing guess is reasonable rather than random.

Take those sentences to a picture of a chair bolted to a roof and they are worse than nothing — sixty
seconds describing something that is not on your screen. Content and Task Fulfillment are both
looking at fit, and fit is what a memorised answer cannot carry. Beneath all of it sits the floor: a
response giving **insufficient information to assess** scores 0 or 1, and levels 0 and 1 map to no
CLB level at all.

What travels is the habit, not the words: name what it resembles, say what is wrong with the
resemblance, give size and material, move in one direction, guess at the purpose, never point.
