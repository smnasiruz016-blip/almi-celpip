## Your English is probably not the problem

**Most people who are stuck on CELPIP Writing are stuck for reasons that have nothing to do with
their English.** They miss a demand in the prompt. They write a template. They spend twenty minutes
polishing Task 1 and eight minutes on Task 2.

Each of those costs marks that better grammar cannot buy back — and the harshest rule in the test
is waiting:

> A response giving **insufficient information to assess** scores **0 or 1**.

This page is a diagnostic, not a lesson. Four failure modes — how to recognise each in writing you
have already done, what it really is, the fix.

## The failure: treating a marking problem as a language problem

You get a Writing level lower than you expected, so you buy a grammar book. Another month, another
attempt, the same level — because nobody checked the diagnosis.

Writing is **53 minutes and two tasks** — Task 1 an email, Task 2 a survey response. **All four
failures below are visible in your own drafts without a rater, and none is fixed by better
sentences.** Open three answers you have already written — you will need them.

| Failure | How you recognise it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| The missed demand | Count what the prompt asked, then what you answered | 30 seconds counting, 30 checking |
| The template | Your openings are interchangeable between prompts | Write to this prompt, not from memory |
| The split | Check the clock — Task 2 gets a third of your time | A hard switch at the halfway point |
| Vagueness | Sentences you could have written without reading the prompt | One concrete detail per paragraph |

## 1. The missed demand

**Recognise it:** in each of three old answers, count the things the prompt asked for, then the
things you answered — with a finger on the screen, not from memory, because this failure feels fine
from the inside. Paragon's own Task 1 sample gives exactly **three bullets** the email must cover, so
the count is small and checkable.

**What it is:** the most expensive mistake available on this test, because it is not a deduction — it
is a floor. On CELPIP's chart, 0 and 1 carry the "insufficient information to assess" label and map
to no Canadian Language Benchmark level at all. Fluent writing about two of three demands is not a
good answer with a penalty applied.

**The fix:** thirty seconds before you write — count the demands, write the number down with a
one-word tag for each — and thirty seconds after, pointing at each tag to find the sentence that
answers it. A tag with no sentence is the best thing you can find with time still on the clock.

## 2. The template

**Recognise it:** put two answers to different prompts side by side. If the openings could be swapped
without anyone noticing, you have a template. Now cover the first and last paragraph of each and read
the middle. If that would fit almost any question too, the template is not decorating your answer —
it is your answer.

**What it is:** a memorised skeleton is prompt-independent by design — the entire selling point, and
exactly what makes it both detectable and empty. **A template can carry a greeting and a sign-off. It
cannot carry content — and content is what is marked.** The sentences showing you read this situation
and answered this request are the ones a template has no way to supply.

**The fix:** keep what convention genuinely fixes — a greeting, a sign-off — and write everything
between them to this prompt. If you cannot start a body paragraph without your skeleton, that is a
planning problem, and counting the demands fixes more of it than a memorised phrase can.

This page deliberately publishes no template: warning you about templates and then handing you one
would only change whose skeleton you carry.

## 3. The split

**Recognise it:** check the clock next time you practise and write the two numbers down. Two tasks,
**53 minutes.** If Task 2 routinely gets a third of your time, its marks get a third of your
attention — and your Writing level comes from the section, not from your better task.

**What it is:** sequence, not laziness. Task 1 comes first, polishing feels productive, and the cost
lands on a task you have not read yet.

**The fix:** a hard switch at the halfway point, mid-sentence if that is where you are. An unpolished
Task 1 that covered every bullet beats a beautiful one paid for with a rushed Task 2.

**Be clear about what that rule is: it is yours, not the test's.** No official per-task time limit is
published — 53 minutes is the section total, so the halfway point is a discipline you impose. Impose
it anyway — nothing on the screen will do it for you.

## 4. Vagueness

**Recognise it:** ask whether your sentences could have been written by someone who never saw the
prompt. "It is very inconvenient" could. "It costs me fifteen dollars and most of a Saturday" could
not.

**What it is:** the specifics are the part of your answer a rater cannot get from anyone else's.
Vague writing is often grammatically perfect, which is why it survives so much self-editing — you
keep proofreading the sentence instead of asking what it says.

**The fix:** one concrete detail per paragraph — a number, a name, a date, a consequence. In Task 2
it turns "option A is better for the community" into a reason someone could argue with, which is what
a defended choice looks like.

## What nobody can tell you — including this page

Listening and Reading have answer keys — 38 questions each, reported against a raw score out of 38.

**Writing has no key.** Both tasks are prose, and no page — this one included — can tell you the band
an answer would score. A sample captioned "this is a 9" is an opinion, not a measurement.

What you can do without a rater is remove the four failures above. Each is checkable from your desk:
a count, a clock reading, a side-by-side comparison, a hunt for one detail per paragraph. A smaller
claim than "reach level 9" — and unlike that one, you can verify it yourself.

## What actually costs you

- Answering two of three bullets, fluently, and never counting.
- Rereading at the end for grammar instead of for coverage.
- An opening paragraph you memorised before you read the prompt it is sitting on.
- Letting Task 1 run long because it is the one already in front of you.
- Studying grammar for a month without checking which failure cost you the marks.
- Watching the on-screen word counter while you compose — it is for one glance at the end, against
  Paragon's "about 150-200 words". Spell-check goes last too.
