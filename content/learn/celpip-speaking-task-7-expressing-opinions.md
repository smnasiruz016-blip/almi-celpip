## What Task 7 actually asks

A statement is put to you — usually something people genuinely disagree about — and you say whether
you agree, and why.

The "why" is the entire task.

## The failure: saying it again, slower

> *"I think mobile phones should not be allowed in schools. I believe they are not a good thing for
> students. In my opinion, schools are better without them. Phones cause many problems, so I think
> they should be banned."*

Four sentences. **One idea, repeated four times.** No reason has been given at all — only the opinion,
rephrased.

This is the single commonest shape of a weak Task 7, and it is easy to fall into because it *feels*
like elaborating. It is not. Elaborating means adding something new.

## The test for a real reason

**Could a reasonable person disagree with it?**

- *"Phones cause problems"* — nobody can disagree, because nothing specific was claimed. Not a reason.
- *"A teacher spends the first ten minutes of every class policing phones, and that's forty minutes a
  week gone"* — someone could disagree with this. They could say it is exaggerated, or that the
  policing is bad teaching. **That is what makes it a reason.**

If your sentence is too safe to argue with, it is too empty to score.

## The shape that works

1. **Say your position in the first sentence.** Plainly. No *"well, this is a complicated topic…"*
2. **Give two or three reasons, and make them different from each other.** Not three angles on one
   idea — three ideas.
3. **Make one of them concrete.** A number, an example, something that happened.
4. **Concede one point to the other side, and answer it.** This is where the strongest answers
   separate from the merely fluent.
5. **Close by restating the position.** One sentence.

## A worked example

**The prompt** *(original AlmiWorld practice prompt)*

> Some people think workplaces should let employees work from home permanently. Others think staff
> should be in the office. What do you think?

**The response** *(spoken, about 60 seconds)*

> I think most workplaces should let people choose, but I'd stop short of fully remote.
>
> My main reason is that some things only happen by accident. When I worked in an office, half of
> what I learned came from overhearing the person next to me handle a problem I hadn't hit yet.
> Nobody schedules that. On a video call you get the meeting and nothing around it.
>
> The second reason is fairness in a different direction. Working from home is not equal — if you
> have a spare room, it's brilliant; if you have three kids and a kitchen table, it's punishment. An
> office at least gives everyone the same desk.
>
> Now, the argument on the other side is commuting, and it's a strong one. Two hours a day is real
> time and real money. That's exactly why I wouldn't force five days — two or three in the office
> gets most of the benefit at half the cost.
>
> So: choice, but not fully remote.

**Why this works.**

- **The position is in the first sentence, and it is specific.** Not "it depends" — a stated middle
  position with a boundary.
- **The two reasons are genuinely different.** One is about learning; one is about fairness. Neither
  is a restatement of the other.
- **One is concrete and personal.** The overhearing example is the sentence a marker remembers.
- **The concession is real.** Commuting is granted as *"a strong one"* — and then used to justify the
  exact position taken. That is argument, not hedging.

## Middle positions are allowed — but they must have edges

*"It depends on the situation"* is not a position. **"Choice, but not fully remote"** is, because it
rules something out.

If you take a middle line, say what you would refuse. A middle position with no edges is the same
empty answer as no position at all — and CELPIP's harshest rule is waiting:

> A response giving **insufficient information to assess** scores **0 or 1**.

## What is not being marked

- **Not whether your opinion is popular, or right.** There is no key.
- **Not your accent.**
- **Not whether it is your real view.** Argue the side you can build fastest.

## The habit that fixes this task

After each sentence you practise, ask: **could someone argue with that?** If not, delete it and say
something they could. Three arguable sentences beat a minute of agreeable ones.
