## Two jobs in one task

Task 5 gives you two options — usually two things you might buy, book or choose — and a **person** you
are talking to. You compare the options, then you persuade that person to take one.

**That is two separate jobs**, and the second one is where the marks are.

Most answers do the first job well and then stop. They lay the two options side by side, note that
both have advantages, and run out of time. It sounds thorough. It is half a task.

## The person is not decoration

Task 5 always tells you something about who you are persuading. They are on a budget. They have small
children. They do not drive. They hate crowds.

**That detail is the task.** A comparison that would suit anybody is not persuasion — it is a product
review.

Read these two:

> *"The second apartment is bigger and it has a balcony, which is nice. The first one is closer to
> downtown."*

> *"Take the second one, Mum. You said the stairs were the problem at the last place — this one's
> ground floor, and the bus to the clinic stops right outside."*

The first is a comparison. The second is persuasion, and it is persuasion **because it uses what we
were told about her.**

## The shape that works

1. **Name your choice in the first sentence.** Not at the end. The person needs to know what you are
   arguing for before you argue.
2. **Give the comparison in service of the choice** — not as a neutral summary. Each point should
   explain why yours wins.
3. **Use at least two facts about the person.** This is the difference between scoring and not.
4. **Handle their likely objection.** They will have one. Name it and answer it.
5. **Close by asking them to act.** *"Let's book it tonight before it goes."*

## A worked example

**The prompt** *(original AlmiWorld practice prompt)*

> Your friend Miriam wants a short holiday. She has two weeks off, a small budget, and does not enjoy
> flying. Option A is a week in Mexico. Option B is a week driving through Nova Scotia. Persuade her
> to choose one.

**The response** *(spoken, about 60 seconds)*

> Do Nova Scotia, Miriam.
>
> I know Mexico sounds like the bigger trip, but you told me flying is the part you dread — and this
> way there's no airport at all. You just get in the car and go. Two weeks off means you can take
> your time and stop wherever you feel like.
>
> And it's kinder to the budget. No flights, no resort prices. You're paying for gas and small places
> to stay, and you can cook half your meals if you want to.
>
> The thing you'd give up is the weather, and I'm not going to pretend the Atlantic in October is the
> Caribbean. But you'd be driving the coast, eating in fishing towns, and stopping when you see
> something. That's much more your kind of holiday than a hotel pool.
>
> Have a look at the ferry times this evening and let's plan the route.

**Why this works.**

- **Sentence one is the choice.**
- **Every comparison serves the argument.** The flight, the budget, the pace — all three come from
  what we were told about Miriam.
- **The objection is faced.** The weather is named honestly and then answered.
- **It ends with an action.** Not "so, that's my opinion" — a thing to do tonight.

## What is not being marked

- **Not which option you chose.** Arguing for Mexico would score identically if it were argued from
  Miriam's actual situation.
- **Not your accent.**
- **Not fairness.** This is the one task where balance actively hurts you. You are not writing a
  review; you are talking someone into something.

And underneath:

> A response giving **insufficient information to assess** scores **0 or 1**.

A minute of even-handed comparison with no recommendation can land there.

## The habit that fixes this task

**Say the choice out loud before you plan anything else.** With under two minutes per task including
preparation, deciding while you speak is normal — but deciding *whether to decide* is fatal. Pick in
the first two seconds, say it, and build the case as you go.
