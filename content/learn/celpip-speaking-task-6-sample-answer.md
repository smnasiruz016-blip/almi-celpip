Six of the eight Speaking tasks give you thirty seconds to prepare. Task 6 gives you sixty.

That difference is the most useful thing anyone can tell you about this task. **A minute is not
extra time to polish your wording — it is time to decide, because Task 6 hands you two options and
refuses to say which is right.** Both let somebody down. You get sixty seconds to pick one and
sixty seconds to tell a named person what you picked.

## The task

"Dealing with a Difficult Situation" is task 6 of 8 in the Speaking component, which runs **15
minutes** in total. It gives you **60 seconds of preparation and 60 seconds of speaking** — one of
only two tasks with a full minute of prep. Those per-task times are official, but they come from
Paragon's Speaking study pack, not from the test-format page, which lists the task names without
the clock. That is why so few guides quote them.

Sixty seconds of speech is roughly **130 to 160 words** — enough for a decision, a reason, an offer
and an apology, and not enough for a warm-up.

## The prompt

*(original AlmiWorld practice prompt)*

> You rent out the basement suite of your house. Your tenant, Marielle, has lived there for two
> years and her lease ends on 30 September. Your father is having hip surgery in September and
> cannot manage stairs, so he needs the basement.
>
> You can renew Marielle's lease and tell your father he has to stay where he is — or you can end
> the lease and ask Marielle to move out. Marielle has not answered her phone all day.
>
> Leave a voicemail for Marielle.

Note what the prompt withholds: your father's name, the rent, whether you have a spare room. **You
are allowed to invent all of it, and you almost have to.** Situations are resolved with specifics —
a date, an amount, an offer. Inventing them is not cheating. It is the task.

## Sixty seconds of preparation, honestly

Three decisions, and none of them is about vocabulary.

**Who loses.** Father or tenant. Pick in the first ten seconds, because a speaker still deciding at
second forty will hedge for the whole minute.

**What you can offer.** The decision is fixed, so the only place left to be generous is the
softening. A free month, a reference letter, help with the truck — one of these, invented on the
spot, beats any amount of gentle phrasing.

**Where the apology goes.** Once, late. An apology in the opening line becomes the news, and the
real news then arrives to a listener who has already relaxed.

## The answer

> Marielle, hi, it's Tomas. I tried you twice today, so I'm just going to leave this. I've got bad
> news and I'd rather you hear it from me now than get a letter — I'm not renewing the suite for
> October. It's decided, I've already told my dad. He's having his hip replaced on the ninth and
> he can't do stairs after, so he's moving into the basement. That's the whole reason. It's not
> you.
>
> Here's what I can do, though. October's rent-free — stay the month, look properly, don't rush
> it. And I'll write you a reference: two years, never once late, I'll say exactly that.
>
> And honestly, I'm not going to pretend October's a decent month to be hunting for a place in
> this city. It isn't. That part's on me for the timing.
>
> I'm sorry, Marielle. Call me tonight, any time, and we'll sort out the truck.

*Approximately 155 words — about 60 seconds at a normal speaking pace.*

## Read against the four dimensions

CELPIP publishes four rating dimensions for Speaking: **Content/Coherence, Vocabulary,
Listenability and Task Fulfillment**. What follows is not a score. **Nobody outside Paragon can put
a level on a sample answer — there is no answer key for Speaking, no published weighting for the
four dimensions, and no rater in this room.** What follows points at what each dimension looks at
and where this answer supplies it.

**Content/Coherence.** The order is doing work. Bad news, reason, offer, honest bit, apology — each
block short, each finished before the next starts. "It's decided, I've already told my dad" closes
the question of whether this is negotiable, which is what lets everything after it be generous
instead of vague.

**Vocabulary.** Ordinary and exact, which is the useful combination. "Rent-free", "reference", "on
the ninth", "never once late". None of it is impressive and all of it is precise, and precision is
what a listener actually registers.

**Listenability** is officially defined as *"How easy is it to listen to and understand the
response?"*, with sub-points including *"Rhythm, pronunciation, and intonation"*. Short sentences
help. "That's the whole reason. It's not you." is two beats you can land cleanly, where one long
subordinate sentence would flatten out.

**Task Fulfillment.** The answer chooses, says which way it chose, and gives Marielle something she
can act on tonight. Beneath this dimension sits the harshest rule in CELPIP: a response giving
**insufficient information to assess** scores **0 or 1**. A minute of sympathetic noises with no
decision in it is precisely what that rule describes.

## What this answer is not

It is not a shape you can carry to another prompt. **This answer works because of its details, and
its details are the part you cannot borrow.**

"He's having his hip replaced on the ninth and he can't do stairs after" exists only because this
prompt has a basement in it. "Two years, never once late" only lands because the prompt gave
Marielle two years. "October's rent-free" fits a lease that ends on 30 September — move the prompt
to a cancelled wedding booking or a shift you cannot cover and all three sentences are dead weight.

A reader who memorises them will produce a worse answer on a different prompt, not a better one,
because the fit is where the marks live. A frame you brought with you cannot contain anything
specific to a prompt you had not seen yet.

## What actually costs you

- **Being kind instead of being clear.** Sixty seconds of apology with no decision in it fails the
  task, however pleasant it sounds.
- **Delivering the news late.** If it arrives at second forty, there is no time left to soften it,
  and softening was the only part you controlled.
- **Apologising three times.** It reads as unwillingness to own the decision, and it eats the
  seconds your offer needed.
- **Refusing to invent.** Waiting for the prompt to supply a detail it was never going to supply.
- **Pretending the bad option is fine.** One honest sentence beats a paragraph of reassurance.
