## What Part 3 actually is

**Part 3 is the only part of the Reading test with a fifth option — E, for information that is not
stated in the reading.** The official study material is explicit: *"Choose E if the information is
not stated in the reading."* Nowhere else in CELPIP Reading can the correct answer be *this was
never said*. You are not only choosing which paragraph a statement belongs to — you are deciding
whether it belongs anywhere at all.

The rest is plain: **"a four-paragraph informational text about a person, place, event, or
process"**, nine questions, no second text — the only Reading part that is a single passage with no
email attached. Register: **"formal, educational, descriptive"**. The difficulty is all in the
not-stated decision.

Paragon's published procedure for this part is four steps: use the paragraph topics to find the
relevant paragraph, scan it for the information, read that part carefully to check the exact
information is there — and *"Choose E if the information is not stated in the reading."* Paragon
does not publish how the four paragraphs are labelled on the real screen, so this page letters them
**A to D** for clarity. Treat those letters as ours, not as the test's.

## The failure: treating E as a confession

Under time pressure, "not stated" feels like giving up. Choosing a paragraph feels like *reading*;
choosing E feels like admitting you could not find it. So you scan again, take the paragraph that
sounds closest, and place the statement there — onto a claim the text never made.

Then somebody warns you about that and the failure inverts: anything you cannot locate in eight
seconds becomes E, because E is where uncertainty goes.

**E has the same standing as A, B, C and D, and it is right or wrong for the same reason they are:
what the text says.** A statement placed in paragraph 2 because paragraph 2 *felt* related is not a
safer answer — it is a wrong answer that took longer.

## Worked example *(original AlmiWorld practice item)*

> **A.** The Rosewood Seed Library occupies one oak cabinet in the reading room of a prairie branch
> library. Its collection is not books but seed — roughly 240 varieties, held in paper envelopes in
> the drawers of a card catalogue decommissioned in 1998. Membership is free with a library card.
>
> **B.** Borrowing follows an unusual procedure. A member takes up to six envelopes a season and
> records each on a paper slip. No due date is attached. Members are asked
> instead to grow the seed, let part of the crop mature past the eating stage, and bring back seed
> from the best plants. In a typical year, about a third of the varieties come back.
>
> **C.** The collection's value lies in local adaptation. Seed from plants that survived a particular
> soil, pest year and frost date carries traits suited to that place. Volunteers therefore label
> every return with its year and neighbourhood. Varieties that fail repeatedly are retired from the
> drawers.
>
> **D.** The model has since been copied by four other branches, and the collection is now consulted
> by growers outside the region. Two paid staff hours a week support the work; everything else is
> done by members.

**Statement 1: The library's seed is kept in furniture that once served another purpose.**

Paragraph **A**. No content word of the statement appears in the passage — but a decommissioned
card catalogue holding seed envelopes is exactly that. **A paraphrase you have to translate is
still stated.**

**Statement 2: Most members return seed at the end of the growing season.**

**E** — the item that decides your score. Paragraph B has the vocabulary, the timing, the subject
and a number. Everything except the claim.

- **B is wrong** because the passage says members are *asked* to bring seed back, and that *about a
  third of the varieties* come back. A third of the varieties is not most members: one counts
  varieties, the other people — and a third is not most either way. Two small drifts, both fatal.
- **A is wrong** — it covers what the collection is and who may join, not what members do.
- **C is wrong** even though returns appear there. Paragraph C *labels* returns; it never counts
  them.
- **D is wrong** — it is about the model spreading and who staffs it.

**Statement 3: The library is funded by an annual grant from the city.**

Also **E**. Paragraph D says two paid staff hours a week support the work. Paid hours imply a
budget, a budget implies a source, a municipal grant is the obvious source — and the passage never
says it.

That is the distinction the whole part is built on. **Implied strongly is not stated.** A paragraph
can leave you certain of something it never said — that is competent writing doing its job. Your
task is not to report what a careful reader would conclude, but what is on the page.

Two of the three are E. Nothing tells you how many of the nine will be — a quota of one or two came
from you, not from the test.

## The shape that works

1. **Read all four paragraphs first, then label them.** One phrase each — *what it is*, *how
   borrowing works*, *why local seed matters*, *where it spread*. That map turns nine statements
   into nine lookups.
2. **Reduce each statement to its claim, then hunt the claim, not the vocabulary.** Subject, verb,
   quantity. Shared words are how the trap is built — the paragraph that sounds nearest is a
   candidate, not an answer.
3. **Check quantities word by word** — *most, some, all, about a third, usually, must, may, asked
   to.* A scope shift you read past is how a nearly-stated statement catches you.
4. **Answer every one.** There are **no points deducted for wrong answers**, so a blank scores what
   a wrong guess scores. Reading is timed per part and **"you can't go back to a previous part."**

## The E procedure: two checks, both required

Before you may **choose E**, you must have:

- named the gap between the nearest paragraph and the statement — a quantity, a subject, a cause, a
  modal verb. "I could not find it" is not a gap. **If you cannot say what is different, you have
  not checked.**
- confirmed the claim is not sitting elsewhere in other words. Statements get paraphrased; missing
  vocabulary is evidence of nothing.

Before you may **reject E**, you must have:

- found the sentence, not the topic. Point at it. A paragraph being *about* the subject is not the
  same as it *containing* the claim.
- checked that you are not supplying the link. Did the text say this, or did I finish it? If the
  connection runs through a step you added, the answer is E.

## What actually costs you the mark

- Placing a statement in the paragraph that shares the most words with it.
- Reading "asked to" as "do", "may" as "must", "about a third" as "most".
- Choosing E because you ran out of patience, not because you found the discrepancy.
- Refusing E because two of your answers are already E.
- Answering an inference. The text made you believe it; that is not the text saying it.
- Leaving a statement blank. Nothing is deducted for a wrong answer, so a blank is a mark declined.
