## The trap is built into the order

Task 3 shows you an image and asks you to describe it. **Task 4 shows you the same image and asks
what will happen next.**

You have just spent a minute describing. Your mouth is warmed up for describing. And the image is
still in front of you. So most people describe it again, with a few future-tense words sprinkled in,
and score badly on a task they understood perfectly.

**Task 3 is the present. Task 4 is the future.** Nothing you say in Task 4 should be visible in the
picture.

## What a weak answer sounds like

> *"The children are playing on the floor and the mother is working on her laptop. Maybe she will
> continue working. The dog is near the mess and maybe it will stay there. The father is cooking so
> probably he will finish the food."*

Every sentence begins in the picture and barely leaves it. *"He is cooking, so he will finish
cooking"* is not a prediction — it is a description with a future tense bolted on.

A prediction says something the picture **does not show.**

## What a strong answer sounds like

> *"I think the mother is about to notice the cereal on the floor, and the moment she does, the
> laptop is getting closed. She'll probably deal with the older child first, because he's the one
> who looks like he already knows he's in trouble.
>
> The dog is going to get to that cereal before anyone cleans it up — it's already too close. So I'd
> guess someone ends up moving the dog out of the kitchen before they can start sweeping.
>
> As for the father, I don't think he's noticed any of it yet. He's got his back to the room. I'd
> say breakfast is going to be about ten minutes later than he's planning."*

Nothing there is in the photograph. Every sentence is a consequence.

## The shape that works

1. **Start with the most likely next event.** The one thing that is clearly about to happen.
2. **Say what follows from it** — a chain, not a list. *"She notices → the laptop closes → she deals
   with the older one first."*
3. **Give a reason for at least one prediction.** *"Because he looks like he already knows."* This is
   what separates predicting from guessing.
4. **Predict something about a person who has not noticed anything.** This almost always produces the
   most interesting sentence in the answer.

## Commit

**Hedging is what costs marks here**, and it is the natural instinct. Nobody can know the future, so
people protect themselves: *"maybe… perhaps… it might be possible that…"*

Six hedges in sixty seconds is not caution. It is a response that never says anything.

The fix is not to become certain. It is to use **confident language about uncertain things**, which
is what fluent speakers actually do:

- *"I think she's about to…"*
- *"That dog is getting to the cereal first."*
- *"He'll probably…"*
- *"I'd guess…"*
- *"By the time he turns around…"*

Each of those is honest. None of them is timid.

## Useful phrases for the future

- *is about to / is going to / any second now*
- *by the time he turns around / once she sees it*
- *ends up doing / will have to*
- *my guess is / I'd say / knowing kids that age*

Notice how many carry a **sequence**. Task 4 rewards a chain of events, and these are the words that
build one.

## What is not being marked

- **Not whether you are right.** Nobody knows what happened next. There is no photograph of the next
  minute.
- **Not your accent.**
- **Not how many predictions you make.** Three connected predictions beat eight disconnected ones.

And the rule underneath everything:

> A response giving **insufficient information to assess** scores **0 or 1**.

A minute of re-description can reach that. If a marker cannot find a prediction in your answer, you
did not do the task.

## One habit to practise

Do Task 3 and Task 4 on the same photo, back to back, exactly as the test does. **Then check whether
a single sentence appears in both.** If one does, that is the sentence costing you the mark — and
catching it in practice is the entire skill.
