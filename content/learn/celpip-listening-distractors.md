**The wrong options are not random, and they are not there to be silly.** A well-built
multiple-choice item gives you three wrong answers that a person who understood most of the audio
could reasonably pick. That means "listen more carefully" is not the fix — the fix is knowing what
the wrong ones are made of, so that when two options both feel right you know which question to
ask.

This matters more in Listening than people expect, because **you read the answer options — they are
not spoken to you.** The official format page is explicit that test takers "will be required to
read answer choices in the Listening Test". Every one of the 38 questions is therefore part
listening problem and part multiple-choice problem, and the second half is where prepared people
quietly gain marks.

## The failure

Truth-checking. You read an option, ask yourself *did the audio say this?*, find that it did, and
click it.

That instinct is exactly what the wrong options are built to catch. A good distractor is usually
**true about the clip**. It fails on something else — relevance, ownership, timing, or direction.
Four shapes cover almost all of them.

---

## 1. True but not the answer

The option states something genuinely said in the clip. It just does not answer the question that
was asked.

*(original AlmiWorld practice item)*

> **Priya:** The installers can start Monday if that suits you.
> **Dan:** Monday's the problem — the flooring crew is still in the unit until Wednesday.
> **Priya:** Thursday, then. It's a two-day job either way.

**Why can't the installation begin on Monday?**

- **A.** The job takes two days.
- **B.** Another crew is still working in the unit.

A is accurate. Priya says it, in those words, seconds before you answer. But it answers *how long*,
not *why not Monday*. B is the answer.

**Test: read the question again, not the options.** When two options both sound heard, the tie is
never broken by going back over the options — it is broken by re-reading the question stem and
noticing which word it turns on. *Why. When. Who. What will they do next.*

## 2. Right detail, wrong owner

In any part with two or more speakers, a view or a fact gets detached from the person who said it
and attached to someone else. Part 1 has two speakers, Part 5 has three — and Part 5 is a **video
clip**, which is your best defence here if your eyes are up.

*(original AlmiWorld practice item)*

> **Omar:** I'd put the money into the delivery vans. They're what customers actually see.
> **Lena:** I'd fix the ordering system first. The vans can survive another year.
> **Omar:** Fair — the system does go down about once a week.

**What does Lena think the company should pay for first?**

- **A.** New delivery vans.
- **B.** The ordering system.

A is a real opinion, expressed clearly, and it is Omar's. Nothing about A is false except the name
in the question.

**Test: name the speaker before you check the content.** Say the name to yourself first — *Lena* —
and only then read the option. Reverse that order and the familiar phrase "delivery vans" will pull
you in before you have checked whose it was. In your notes, never write a claim without a letter or
initial in front of it.

## 3. The pre-correction value

Someone states a detail and it is corrected or revised later in the same clip. The wrong option is
the first version — the one that had a few seconds alone in your head before the fix arrived.

*(original AlmiWorld practice item)*

> **Caller:** The evening workshop is forty dollars, isn't it?
> **Clerk:** It was forty last term. It's forty-five now — the room hire went up in April.
> **Caller:** Forty-five. All right, put me down.

**How much will the caller pay?**

- **A.** $40
- **B.** $45

Forty is said twice and is the number you heard first. It is also dead.

**Test: was this detail ever mentioned twice?** A number, date or plan that comes back a second
time is almost never repetition — it is revision. Listen for the correction frame: *it was, it's
actually, sorry, I meant, as of last month, that's changed.* When you take notes, don't erase the
first value. Strike it and write the new one beside it, so at answer time you can see that the item
was contested and which version won.

## 4. The concession, not the conclusion

In a viewpoints monologue or a discussion, a speaker grants the other side a point before rejecting
it. The wrong option quotes the concession accurately. Part 6 — "Listening to Viewpoints" on the
format page, "Listening for Viewpoints" in the study pack and guidebook — is one speaker for about
three minutes, and this is its house trap.

*(original AlmiWorld practice item)*

> Nobody disputes that the bike lanes cost more per kilometre than the city predicted, and the
> construction made last winter miserable for drivers on Third Avenue. That is real, and dismissing
> it does the project no favours. But the street now moves more people through it at rush hour than
> it did with four lanes of cars — and that is the number the council should be judged on.

**What is the speaker's position on the bike lanes?**

- **A.** The cost overruns outweigh the benefits.
- **B.** The lanes are worth it because the street moves more people.

A is a faithful summary of the first two sentences. Those sentences are not the speaker's view —
they are the view the speaker is about to overrule.

**Test: did the sentence come before or after the turn?** Every concession has a hinge: *but, even
so, still, that said, what that misses, the trouble with that argument.* Mark the hinge in your
notes with a single slash. Everything before it is somebody else's position, borrowed. Everything
after it is the speaker's.

## The shape that works

Four questions, in this order, whenever two options both feel heard:

1. **What is the question actually asking?** Re-read the stem, not the options.
2. **Whose opinion is this?** Name the speaker before you read the content.
3. **Was this detail revised?** If a number or plan appeared twice, take the later one.
4. **Is this before or after the hinge?** Concession, or conclusion.

This runs in a few seconds once it is habitual, which is the point of practising it now rather than
on test day. **All audio clips are played one time only**, so there is no second pass to fall back
on — the clip is gone and only the options are left.

## How to use this when you review your own practice

Scoring a practice set tells you how many. It does not tell you what.

After each wrong answer, go back to **the option you chose** — not the one you should have chosen —
and name which of the four it was. Write the number in the margin: 1, 2, 3 or 4. Some will be none
of them, which is genuine mishearing, and that is a useful category too.

After about ten wrong items you have a distribution, and the distribution is the instruction.

- Mostly 1s: a reading-speed and question-parsing problem. Practise re-reading stems, not more
  clips.
- Mostly 2s: your notes are not attributing. Add initials to everything.
- Mostly 3s: you are locking in an answer before the clip is finished.
- Mostly 4s: you are summarising the first half of arguments. Work Part 6 specifically.

Re-run the count every couple of weeks. The shape you are losing to changes as you improve, and
what you practise should change with it.

## What actually costs you the mark

- Checking whether an option was *said* instead of whether it *answers*.
- Notes with opinions in them and no names attached.
- Committing to a number the moment you hear it, while the speaker is still talking.
- Treating a concession as a position because it arrived first and sounded definite.
- Reviewing which questions you got wrong without ever looking at which wrong option pulled you in.
- Practising with a pause button, then meeting a clip that plays once and cannot be replayed.
