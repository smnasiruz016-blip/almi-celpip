You can name every object in the picture, correctly, in good English, and still give a thin answer.
That's the strange thing about Task 3: the picture isn't a vocabulary quiz. **The task is to make
someone who cannot see the picture understand what is going on in it** — and a list of objects never
does that.

## The failure

Naming instead of describing. It sounds like this: *"There is a woman. There is a machine. There is a
table. There are some towels."* Everything in that is true and none of it is a scene — nobody is doing
anything, nothing relates to anything else, and the sixty seconds run out around the fourth noun with
the room still invisible. The fix isn't a bigger word list. It's saying what the whole thing looks
like before you go near the details, and describing people by what they are *doing*.

## The task

Task 3 is officially called **"Describing a Scene"** — one of eight tasks in a Speaking component
that runs **15 minutes** in total. An image appears, and you describe it. You get **30 seconds of
preparation and 60 seconds of speaking** — roughly 130 to 160 words at a normal talking speed. Those
per-task times come from Paragon's Speaking study pack, not the test-format page, which gives the
task names and component length but not the individual clocks — which is why few sites quote them.

## The prompt

> **(original AlmiWorld practice prompt)** Describe the picture to someone who cannot see it. You have
> 30 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to speak.

*The picture you are looking at, in words, because this page can't show it to you:* **a small
neighbourhood laundromat on a grey, rainy afternoon.** *Front left, a woman in her thirties in a
yellow rain jacket is pushing clothes into an open front-loading washer, and a toddler in a stroller
beside her leans sideways with one hand out towards the machine's round glass door. In the middle of
the room, an older man in a cardigan has spread his laundry across the whole folding table and is
stacking towels. By the rain-streaked window, a teenager sits on a plastic chair with big headphones
on, a textbook open across her knees and her phone face-down on the sill. At the back, an attendant in
a blue apron leans on the change counter with a phone to his ear. By the door, a dripping umbrella in
a bucket and a corkboard with a hand-drawn "LOST CAT — Pepper" flyer on it, its little tear-off phone
number tabs nearly all gone.*

## Thirty seconds of preparation, honestly

Thirty seconds is one deep breath and three decisions. What is this place, and what's the weather
doing? Everything else hangs off that. In what order do I cross the room — left to right isn't a rule,
it's just cheaper than jumping around. And **what have I noticed that a careless describer would
skip?** Here, the flyer with the tabs torn off. Then stop planning — you won't finish the room anyway.

## The answer

> Okay, so it looks like a rainy weekday afternoon in a small neighbourhood laundromat, and everybody
> in there is just waiting out their wash. On the left there's a woman in a yellow rain jacket loading
> a front-loader, and her toddler's in the stroller beside her leaning over to watch the drum — well,
> trying to get a hand on the glass, actually. Behind her an older gentleman in a cardigan's taken
> over the whole folding table, and his stack of towels is about this high, so he's clearly been there
> a while. Over by the window a teenager's got big headphones on with a textbook open on her knees,
> and honestly I don't think she's read a word of it. The attendant's on the phone at the change
> counter. And the thing I keep looking at is the lost-cat flyer by the door —
> nearly all the little tear-off tabs are gone, so the whole street must be out looking for that cat.

*About 160 words, spoken in roughly 60 seconds.*

## Read against the four dimensions

CELPIP publishes four Speaking dimensions: **Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability and Task
Fulfillment**. What follows is not a score. **Nobody can put a level on a sample answer** — there's no
answer key for Speaking, no published weighting across the dimensions, and no rater in this room. What
you can do is see what each one looks at, and where this answer supplies it.

**Content/Coherence.** The first sentence does the structural work: *"it looks like a rainy weekday
afternoon in a small neighbourhood laundromat, and everybody in there is just waiting out their
wash."* That's the whole picture in one breath, and every later detail lands inside a frame the
listener already has. The route is steady — left, middle, window, back, door.

**Vocabulary.** The useful words here aren't rare ones, they're precise ones: *front-loader*, *drum*,
*cardigan*, *folding table*, *change counter*, *tear-off tabs*. Precision is doing the work, not
difficulty.

**Listenability.** Officially, *"How easy is it to listen to and understand the response?"*, with
rhythm, pronunciation and intonation among its sub-points. Paragon states plainly that *"Our raters
specifically do not consider your accent when rating your responses"* — but pronunciation does sit
inside the rubric, because the question is whether you're understood. Short clauses joined by *and*
and *so*, as here, are easier to say cleanly than long subordinated ones.

**Task Fulfillment.** The verb was *describe, to someone who cannot see it*. The inferences —
*"he's clearly been in there a while"*, *"I don't think she's read a word of it"* — are the answer
doing that job rather than reciting contents. Beneath this dimension sits the harshest rule on the
test: a response giving **insufficient information to assess scores 0 or 1**, and levels 0 and 1 map
to no CLB level at all.

## What this answer is not

It is not a structure to reuse. **This answer works because of its details, and its details are the
part you cannot borrow.** *"His stack of towels is about this high"* is worth something in front of
this picture and nothing in front of any other — same with the torn-off tabs, and with the toddler
reaching for the glass door.

Memorise those sentences and you'll produce a *worse* answer next time, because you'll spend sixty
seconds steering a real picture towards remembered lines. The marks live in the fit between what you
say and what's in front of you.

## What actually costs you

- Listing objects instead of situations — nouns with no verbs, no relation between them.
- Skipping the opening overview, so the listener assembles a room from fragments.
- Saving your best detail for the end and never reaching it.
- Reciting a remembered frame, which makes this picture look like the last one you practised.
