## What Task 1 actually asks

You are given a short situation: someone you know — a friend, a cousin, a neighbour, a colleague —
has a problem or a decision in front of them. You speak your advice into the microphone.

It is the first of the eight Speaking tasks, and the whole Speaking section is **15 minutes for all
eight**, so you have very little time to think before you begin.

## The failure that costs most people this task

Read these two openings.

> *"I think you should think carefully about it and consider all your options. It's important to make
> the right decision, so take your time and don't rush into anything."*

> *"Take the evening shift, Reza. You said the pay is the same, and the evening one is a ten-minute
> walk from your place."*

The first one is grammatically fine. It is also **advice that would fit any problem ever described to
anyone** — and that is exactly why it scores badly.

A marker reading it cannot tell what the problem was. And CELPIP's harshest rule is waiting:

> A response giving **insufficient information to assess** scores **0 or 1**.

The second opening does three things in twelve words: it **names the person**, it **gives the actual
advice**, and it **uses a specific detail from the prompt**. The marker knows instantly that you
understood the situation.

**Advise the person, not the problem.**

## The shape that works

1. **Say the advice in your first sentence.** Not what you are about to do. The advice itself.
2. **Give two or three reasons, and make each one point at their situation** — a detail from the
   prompt, not a general truth about life.
3. **Deal with the obvious objection.** One sentence. It shows you thought past your own answer.
4. **Close by restating the advice.** Simple, direct, done.

## A worked example

**The prompt** *(original AlmiWorld practice prompt)*

> Your cousin Reza has been offered a second job at a warehouse. The pay is the same as his current
> job. The new job is a ten-minute walk from his apartment; his current job is a fifty-minute bus
> ride. But he has worked at his current job for four years and likes the people there. He has asked
> what you think he should do.

**The response** *(spoken, about 55 seconds)*

> Take the warehouse job, Reza.
>
> The pay is the same, so this really comes down to your time. Right now you're spending nearly two
> hours a day on a bus. Over a week that's ten hours — more than a working day, every week, that you
> get back. You could sleep later, or actually see your kids in the evening.
>
> I know the hardest part is the people. Four years is a long time and you like them. But you're not
> moving to another city — you can still meet them, and honestly, once you're not exhausted from
> commuting you'll have more energy for that, not less.
>
> The one thing I'd do first is ask about the shifts. If the new place wants nights and you're not
> ready for that, it changes things.
>
> But if the shifts are reasonable, take it. Ten minutes' walk versus fifty on a bus isn't a small
> thing.

**Why this works.**

- **The advice is the first three words.** No preamble.
- **Every reason comes from the prompt.** The same pay, the ten minutes, the fifty minutes, the four
  years, the people. Nothing generic.
- **It does the arithmetic.** *"Ten hours — more than a working day, every week"* is not in the
  prompt. It was worked out from what was. That is the kind of thing a marker notices.
- **The objection is faced, not avoided.** The friendship is named as the hardest part and then
  answered.
- **There is a condition.** *"Ask about the shifts first"* is what real advice sounds like — and it
  shows judgement rather than certainty.

## What you do not need

- **You do not need a perfect accent.** Speaking is marked on how well you communicate, not on
  sounding Canadian. Be clear; do not try to be someone else.
- **You do not need to be right.** There is no correct answer. Advising Reza to stay would score just
  as well if it were argued from the same specifics.
- **You do not need to fill every second.** A response that answers fully in fifty seconds and stops
  is stronger than one padded to the buzzer.

## What you do need

**Speed at the start.** With under two minutes per task including thinking time, the single most
valuable habit is deciding your advice within a few seconds and beginning. You can build the reasons
while you talk. You cannot build them in silence.
