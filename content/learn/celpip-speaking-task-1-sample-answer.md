Most Task 1 answers fail in a way that looks like success: fluent, organised, grammatical, and
suitable for absolutely anybody. **Advice that would fit any person is not advice — it is a speech
about a topic, and Task 1 does not ask for a speech.** The sample below could not be given to a
second person, and that is the whole point of publishing it.

## The failure

You get a prompt about a friend with a problem. Thirty seconds later you are talking, and what comes
out is *make a schedule, stay positive, talk to people, don't give up.* Every clause is true. Not one
is about the person in the prompt. **Content/Coherence** and **Task Fulfillment** are two of the four
rating dimensions, and generic advice is thin in both.

## The task

Task 1 is **"Giving Advice"** — the first of the eight Speaking tasks, in a component that runs
**15 minutes**. You get **30 seconds of preparation and 90 seconds of speaking**. That
matters: only Task 1 and Task 7 run that long — every other task gives you 60. An answer that lands
at 130 words is half-empty, not concise.

Those per-task times come from Paragon's Speaking study pack. The test-format page publishes the task
names and the component length but not the individual clocks — which is why few sites quote them.

## The prompt

*(original AlmiWorld practice prompt)*

> Your friend Marisol moved to Saskatoon eight months ago on a two-year contract at a food-processing
> plant. She works 3 p.m. to 11 p.m., Monday to Saturday, and she does not drive. She told you she
> has not made a single friend outside work and is thinking about breaking the contract and going
> home. Give her advice.

## Thirty seconds of preparation, honestly

You are not planning an essay in 30 seconds — you are making two decisions.

First the position: leave or stay? Pick one and open with it. A 90-second answer that hedges spends
20 seconds getting to a point.

Then the constraint the advice has to survive. Here it is the shift: 3 to 11 kills every evening
class, league and meetup in the city. So whatever you suggest has to happen before 3 p.m. or on a
Sunday, and be reachable without a car in a Saskatchewan winter. Anything failing either test is not
advice for Marisol.

## The answer

> Okay, Marisol — don't break the contract. Seriously, don't. You'd owe back the relocation money and
> you'd be job-hunting in February, and anyway that's not what's actually wrong. What's wrong is your
> schedule. And your schedule has a hole in it you're not using. Every morning from about nine until
> two you're awake, you're free, and that's when the city is actually open.
>
> So, two things. First, mornings. The rec centre runs drop-in stuff before noon — badminton, the
> pool, whatever you like — and the people there are mostly people who also can't do evenings. Shift
> workers, retired folks, parents. Go the same day every week, though. Not whenever you feel like it.
> Same day, same time, same faces — that's how a stranger turns into somebody who says hi to you.
>
> Second thing, and honestly this is the bigger one. You already ride the number four to the plant
> with, what, eight people? You've been standing next to Tuyet at that stop for eight months. Ask her
> what she does on Sundays. You've both got Sundays.
>
> And keep it close to home. You don't drive, it's minus twenty-five in January — anything more than
> one bus away, you'll quit by week three. Close beats perfect.
>
> Give it until spring. Then decide.

Roughly 210 words, for 90 seconds — about 140 words a minute, an unhurried talking pace, not a race.

## Read against the four dimensions

The four published Speaking dimensions are **Content/Coherence**, **Vocabulary**, **Listenability**
and **Task Fulfillment**.

**Content/Coherence.** The answer commits in its first four words and never re-opens the question.
There are exactly two pieces of advice, each with a reason attached — mornings, *because* that is
when she is free and other shift workers are out. Tuyet, *because* the friend is already standing
beside her. *"Same day, same time, same faces"* explains why the advice would work, which is not the
same as repeating it.

**Vocabulary.** Nothing rare in it — precision instead: *"relocation money"*, *"drop-in"*, *"shift
workers"*, *"one bus away"*. Concrete words about her life beat impressive words about nothing.

**Listenability.** Officially, *"How easy is it to listen to and understand the response?"*, with
*"Rhythm, pronunciation, and intonation"* among its sub-points. Read the answer aloud and notice the
short units — *"So, two things. First, mornings."* Fragments give your voice somewhere to land. Long
subordinate chains are where intonation flattens and the thread goes. On accent:

> "Our raters specifically do not consider your accent when rating your responses."

Accent is not rated. Being understood is — the level descriptors ask whether *"pronunciation errors
rarely impede communication"*. Not the same claim.

**Task Fulfillment.** The verb was *advise*, and the answer advises: it takes a position on the
decision she raised, gives two actions, and handles the obvious objection — the money, the February
job hunt — before she can raise it. Beneath this dimension sits the harshest rule on the test: a
response giving **insufficient information to assess** scores **0 or 1**, and those levels map to no
CLB level at all.

## What no one can tell you

- **This page puts no band on that answer, and no page honestly can.** There is no answer key for
  Speaking, no published weighting across the dimensions, no rater in the room.
- Paragon's pages do not agree on who marks you: the results page says three to five speaking raters,
  the Score Guidance PDF at least three certified raters, and the FAQ describes an AI-human hybrid
  for **Writing** only. Whether Speaking is AI-scored is not published either way.
- What *is* published is the four dimensions. Aim at those.

## What this answer is not

It is not a structure. Three of its lines exist only because of this prompt: *"You've been standing
next to Tuyet at that stop for eight months"*, *"every morning from about nine until two"*, and
*"it's minus twenty-five in January"*. Delete Marisol's shift and her missing licence and all three
go false.

**This answer works because of its details, and its details are the part you cannot borrow.** Carry
the wording into a prompt about a cousin choosing a phone plan and you are reciting furniture from
someone else's house — fluent, and empty where it counts.

What does carry across is what the answer *does*: commits immediately, names the constraint the
advice must survive, gives a reason with each suggestion, answers the objection, stops on purpose
rather than trailing off. Habits, not lines.
