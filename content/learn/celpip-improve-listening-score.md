**"Practise more" is the answer everyone gives, and it is why people plateau.** Four completely
different problems produce the same disappointing Listening result, and each one needs a different
fix. Doing more practice tests without knowing which one you have is repetition, not improvement —
you get better at what you were already doing.

Listening is 38 questions across six parts. What you get back is a count out of 38 — how many you
missed, never why. The why is the only part you can act on.

## The failure

Treating your practice score as feedback. It isn't feedback. It's a total, and a total hides four
separate mechanisms that look identical on the way out.

Below are the four: how to recognise each one, what it actually is, and what to do about it. Most
people have one dominant mode and a little of a second.

---

## 1. Distractor traps — you understood the audio and still chose wrong

**Recognise it:** when you check your answers, the correct option makes obvious sense the moment you
see it. There is no "I didn't catch that" feeling. You heard the clip fine. You just picked the
other one.

**What it actually is:** a reading and reasoning failure, not a hearing one. **You read the answer
options — they are not spoken to you.** Every question is partly a multiple-choice problem, and the
wrong options are doing work.

**The fix is not listening harder.** It is learning what the wrong options are built from. Label
each wrong option you were tempted by. In practice they cluster into a few shapes:

| Shape | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| True but off-question | Something the audio said — answering a question you weren't asked |
| Right detail, wrong speaker | A real number or opinion, attached to the wrong person |
| The pre-correction value | The first price, date or plan — before the speaker changed it |
| Concession, not conclusion | "Expensive, but I'm doing it anyway" — and you chose "expensive" |

Once you can name the shape, you stop being surprised by it. This is the one mode where re-reading
the options before committing raises your count.

## 2. Note-taking overload — you were writing when the answer was said

**Recognise it:** your notes are long and your score is low. Or you remember the beginning of clips
much better than the end — you can describe the setup and go blank on the outcome.

**What it actually is:** attention spent on your pen instead of the audio. Paper and pen are
provided, which reads as an invitation to transcribe. It is not one. **All audio clips are played
one time only** — so a second spent writing is a second of audio you did not hear, unrecoverably.

**The fix is writing less.** Names, numbers, and who wants what. Nothing in sentences. If you are
writing a phrase, you have already missed the next one. The
[note-taking page](/learn/celpip-listening-note-taking) has the notation and what to leave out.

## 3. Losing the thread mid-clip — one word and the next twenty seconds are gone

**Recognise it:** your errors cluster *after* a point in a clip rather than spreading evenly. The
first questions on a passage are fine and the last two are guesses. This is the signature.

**What it actually is:** not vocabulary. It's the recovery loop. You hit an unfamiliar word or a
moment of panic and spend seconds reconstructing what you missed — during which more audio goes
past unheard, giving you more to reconstruct.

**The fix is deliberate recovery, and it has to be practised.** When you lose a sentence, drop it.
Carry on from the *next* one. A missing sentence costs you at most one question. A stall costs you
the rest of the clip. So when it happens, don't rewind and don't stop — finish the questions with
the hole in place. The skill is tolerating the gap.

It hurts most in long single-voice stretches. Part 6 is one speaker for about three minutes, with no
change of voice to give you a fresh entry point.

## 4. The eyes-down gap — you read the options while the audio is still running

**Recognise it:** you do worse on the parts with more questions. Part 1 (Problem Solving) and Part 5
(Discussion) carry eight questions each — Parts 2 and 4 carry five. If accuracy drops on the heavy
parts, this is very likely you.

**What it actually is:** a direct consequence of a confirmed fact — **you read the answer options,
they are not spoken to you.** Reading is not free. Every glance down while audio is playing is a
stretch you processed at half strength, and more questions means more text on screen.

**The fix** is deciding in advance when your eyes are allowed to move: full attention while the
audio runs, reading in the gaps. Part 5 makes this concrete — it is a **video** with **three
speakers**, and the picture tells you who is talking. Looking down loses you the one cue that keeps
three voices apart.

## Running a practice session so that it diagnoses

Do not just score it. A total is not a diagnosis.

1. Do a full part — or a full test — under real conditions. Audio once. No pausing, no rewinding.
2. Mark it, then go back over every wrong answer and **write down which of the four it was.** Even
   narrowing it to two of them is informative.
3. After roughly ten wrong items you have a distribution.
4. **That distribution is your study plan.** Six of them mode 1 means your evenings go on option
   analysis, not more clips. Mode 3 clustered in Parts 5 and 6 means you practise recovery on long
   passages and leave short conversations alone.

Re-run it every two weeks. When your dominant mode changes, so should what you practise.

## What is not officially published

No official raw-score-to-level cutoff exists. CELPIP labels its own conversion table "Approximate",
and the confident cutoff tables on prep sites are reverse-engineered by third parties — they
disagree with one another. **Track your practice results as a count out of 38 and don't convert
them.** A rising count is real evidence — a converted level is somebody's guess.

One official detail that changes how you play it:

> there are no points deducted for wrong answers

So there is never a reason to leave a question blank.

## What actually costs you

- Doing a fifth practice test before you have diagnosed the first four.
- Reviewing the questions you got wrong but not the wrong *option* you were drawn to.
- Blaming vocabulary for what is actually a recovery problem.
- Writing notes in sentences while the only playthrough of the audio runs past.
- Reading ahead through the options during the audio, then wondering why the heavy parts feel
  worse.
- Practising with a pause button. You cannot go back to a previous part on test day.
