**Most CELPIP study plans are a calendar. This one starts with a measurement, because until you know
which skill is lowest you are guessing at what to study.** Each component gets its own CELPIP Level
— there is no single overall level — and programs read the four levels separately. The score report
prints an Average Score, and Paragon is explicit about what it is not:

> "Average Score is not equivalent to any Canadian Language Benchmark (CLB) level"

So the skill that decides your result is usually your weakest, not your average. Raising a strong
component moves the average and nothing else.

**The second half of the problem: the four skills need genuinely different work, and three of them
are not "more English".** Listening is a note-taking and attention problem. Reading is a clock and a
blanks problem. Writing and Speaking are task-fulfilment problems. What genuinely carries across is
narrower than people hope: Writing and Speaking share three of their four rating dimensions —
Content/Coherence, Vocabulary and Task Fulfillment — so work on those counts twice. Nothing you do
for Listening will help your Reading clock.

## The failure

The failure is studying the average. You spread four weeks evenly, you feel busier every week, and
the component that was lowest in week one is still lowest on test day, because nothing was aimed at
it. Its twin is studying general English when what costs you marks is that you cannot take notes
fast enough while reading four answer options.

## Week zero: measure

Before you plan anything, do one task from each component, honestly, under something like real
conditions. Not a full mock test — one task each, then write down what happened.

For **Listening and Reading, record which trap caught you — not the score.** Six out of eight tells
you nothing you can act on. "My notes stopped when the second speaker started" is a plan. "I left
four blanks unanswered because time ran out" is a different plan. Attention, notes, vocabulary, or
the clock — pick one.

For **Writing and Speaking, record whether you answered every demand in the prompt.** Go back to the
prompt afterwards and count. Paragon's own sample Task 1 email gives exactly three bullets the email
must cover — so the honest question is not "was my English good" but "did I cover all three". The
floor rule sits under everything else: a response giving insufficient information to assess scores 0
or 1, and levels 0 and 1 map to no CLB level at all.

## What actually moves each skill

| Skill | What the format actually does to you | The work that moves it |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | Audio clips are played one time only. You **read** the answer options — they are not spoken. Paper and pen are provided, so your notes are the only record. Part 5 is a video with three speakers; Part 6 is one voice for about three minutes. | Note-taking under one-pass conditions: writing while your eyes are on the options, tracking who said what across three speakers, holding attention through a long monologue. |
| Reading | Timed per part, and you cannot go back to a previous part. **13 of the 38 marks are blanks in a second text** — Parts 1, 2 and 4 each pair a main text with a fill-in section. | Pacing per part, so an overrun in Part 1 cannot be repaid later. Then the blanks format on its own, because roughly a third of the paper is that one task type. |
| Writing | 53 minutes, two tasks. The sample instruction asks for "about 150-200 words". Task 1's prompt lists the things the email must cover. | Covering every bullet inside the word range in half your time budget. Read the prompt twice, list its demands, then write. Task Fulfillment is a named rating dimension. |
| Speaking | 15 minutes, eight tasks. **Six of the eight give you 30 seconds of preparation** (Tasks 5 and 6 differ). | Turning 30 seconds into a plan rather than a script. Learn the eight task types by name, and speak to the end of the clock. |

Those per-part details come from Paragon's free study packs, which carry more format detail than the
test-format page. Rather than repeat them:

## One four-week shape, not the four-week shape

This is one reasonable way to arrange the time, not the correct one, and nothing about it guarantees
a level. Weight it toward whatever week zero told you.

- **Week 1** — format first. Work through each component's parts once so nothing on test day is
  structurally new, and confirm your weakest skill rather than assuming it.
- **Week 2** — roughly half your study time on the weakest component alone, in short daily sessions
  rather than one long one. Keep the other three ticking over with one task each.
- **Week 3** — hold the weakest skill daily, add the second weakest, and start doing tasks to the
  clock rather than to comfort.
- **Week 4** — timed and full-length under conditions, then review slowly. Spend the review on the
  diagnosis, not the score.

**No honest page can tell you how many weeks buy a level.** Paragon publishes no such figure, and
anyone quoting one to you has made it up.

## What actually costs you

- **Repeating full mock tests without diagnosing.** A mock test measures. It does not teach. Three
  mocks with no error analysis is three measurements of the same weakness.
- **Memorising templates.** The argument against them is not that you get punished — **CELPIP
  publishes no statement penalising memorised material, and you should distrust any page claiming it
  does.** The argument is what a template cannot contain: the three specific bullets in this prompt,
  the scene actually in front of you, the particular difficulty in this situation. Task Fulfillment
  is rated, and a template only ever fills the space around the content.
- **Studying the skills you enjoy.** Comfort is a signal that this is not your weakest skill.
- **Tuning your study to a raw-score cutoff.** CELPIP labels its own conversion table "Approximate"
  and publishes no cutoff. The confident tables on prep sites are reverse-engineered by third parties
  and contradict each other.

## The week before

Much of what decides test day needs no studying. From Paragon's test day page: **you must arrive
45 minutes prior to your test**, and check-in closes 15 minutes before it. **"Latecomers will be
denied entrance to the test and will NOT be eligible for a refund"**. Your ID must be original, valid
and unexpired, and must match the registration details you entered, including full name and document
number. And:

> "Your identification is the only personal belonging allowed in the test room."

Check your ID against your registration in week three, not the night before — it is the one item here
you cannot fix on the morning.
