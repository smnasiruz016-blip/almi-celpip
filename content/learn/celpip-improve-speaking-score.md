**On six of the eight Speaking tasks you get thirty seconds of preparation.** Not two minutes —
thirty seconds. The component is 15 minutes for 8 tasks — under two minutes each, preparation
included.

Almost every Speaking problem people describe as "my English isn't good enough under pressure" comes
down to that number. **And it is fixable without improving your English at all.**

## The failure this page prevents

Treating a low Speaking level as a verdict on your English. It usually isn't. It is a verdict on
four recognisable behaviours in front of a microphone, each with a different fix. Practising more
without knowing which is yours is how people sit the test three times and score the same. Record
yourself, then work out which of the four you are doing.

## The clock, task by task

The official per-task times. **They come from the Speaking study packs, not the test-format page**,
which gives the task names and the component length but not the timings — which is why almost nobody
quotes them.

| Task | Preparation | Speaking |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Giving Advice | 30 s | 90 s |
| 2 Talking about a Personal Experience | 30 s | 60 s |
| 3 Describing a Scene | 30 s | 60 s |
| 4 Making Predictions | 30 s | 60 s |
| 5 Comparing and Persuading | two parts — see below | |
| 6 Dealing with a Difficult Situation | 60 s | 60 s |
| 7 Expressing Opinions | 30 s | 90 s |
| 8 Describing an Unusual Situation | 30 s | 60 s |

**Task 5 has a two-part structure that surprises people.** You get 60 seconds of preparation with no
speaking at all, then a further 60 seconds of preparation and 60 seconds of speaking. Expect to talk
at the end of the first minute and the task will throw you.

Speaking is rated on four dimensions: **Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability and Task
Fulfillment.** Listenability is officially defined as *"How easy is it to listen to and understand
the response?"* Nothing official publishes how they are weighted.

## Failure mode 1: deciding while the clock runs

**Recognise it:** record yourself and listen to the first fifteen seconds. If you are still circling
— "well, that's an interesting situation, I think there are many things to consider..." — you have
spent a quarter of a sixty-second answer arriving.

**What it is:** with 30 seconds of preparation you cannot plan an answer, only choose one. Spend the
window hunting for the *best* answer and you arrive at the microphone still deciding — and the
deciding gets recorded.

**The fix:** a first sentence that commits inside the first three seconds — the advice, the choice,
the opinion, stated flatly. "I'd tell her to take the job." "The second apartment is better." There
is no correct answer to find. The remaining fifty-odd seconds support the thing you already said,
which is what Content/Coherence looks at.

## Failure mode 2: the silence

**Recognise it:** your recordings have gaps of three or four seconds while you searched for a word.
You will not remember them — they are on the tape.

**What it is:** there is no examiner in the room. Nobody nods, nobody prompts, nobody fills the gap
while you retrieve the word you wanted. **Silence is what gets recorded** — and Listenability is
about how easy the response is to listen to.

**The fix:** a small set of honest continuation moves while your brain catches up — restate what you
just said in different words, give an example, or say the simpler word. "A place where they fix
cars" costs you nothing next to four seconds of nothing. Stopping for the perfect word trades a
Vocabulary point you might not win for a Listenability problem you definitely created.

## Failure mode 3: answering a nearby question

**Recognise it:** read the prompt again after you record, and check whether you did the verb it
asked for. Advise. Predict. Compare *and* persuade. Decide. Describe a scene to someone who cannot
see it.

**What it is:** the commonest way marks are lost, across all eight tasks. A fluent, well-organised
minute about the topic is not the same as doing what the task asked, and Task Fulfillment is a named
dimension. Behind it sits the floor rule, which outranks the rest of the rubric:

> A response giving **insufficient information to assess** scores **0 or 1**.

Levels 0 and 1 map to no CLB level. That is not reserved for blank recordings — it is what happens
when a rater is not given enough of the task to judge.

**The fix:** spend three of your thirty preparation seconds on the verb, not the topic, and make
your first sentence perform it.

## Failure mode 4: running out early, or being cut off

**Recognise it:** time your recordings against the limits in the table.

**What it is:** **the tasks are not all the same length.** Tasks 1 and 7 give you 90 seconds. Most
give 60. A 45-second answer to a 90-second task is not concise, it is unfinished — half the evidence
a rater needed. At the other end, four planned points in a 60-second task means being cut
mid-sentence with the strongest one unspoken.

**The fix:** knowing which two tasks are long. Practise 1 and 7 at 90 seconds until the extra
half-minute stops feeling like an abyss.

## What is not published

- **No weighting for the four dimensions.** Anyone ranking them for you is guessing.
- **Whether Speaking is AI-scored is unknown.** Paragon's FAQ describes an AI-human hybrid system
  for the *Writing* Test specifically. Nothing official says either way about Speaking. Official
  pages also differ on rater counts — the results page says three to five speaking raters, the Score
  Guidance PDF at least three certified raters.
- **Whether templates or memorised material are penalised is unknown.** CELPIP publishes no
  statement on it, whatever you have read elsewhere.

## How to practise so it diagnoses

Recording is not the practice. Listening back with a pen is. Mark three things: **the second you
committed to an answer**, the number of silences over two seconds, and whether you did the prompt's
verb. Three recordings is enough to see a pattern — one of the four modes will be yours, most likely
the same one every time.

## What actually costs you

- Arriving at the microphone still choosing your answer.
- Stopping dead to retrieve one word instead of saying the simpler one.
- A good answer to a question next door to the one asked.
- Treating every task as sixty seconds when two of them are ninety.
- Expecting to speak at the end of Task 5's first preparation minute.
- Practising without listening back, which trains the habit instead of finding it.
- Chasing "better English" when the problem is the clock.
