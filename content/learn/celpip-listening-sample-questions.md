**Most free CELPIP listening practice gives you an answer key. A key tells you that you were wrong;
it does not tell you what you did.**

The useful thing is the reasoning for every option, including the three you did not choose — because
the option you nearly picked is the one that shows you your habit. Reaching for true-but-irrelevant
detail is a different fault from mis-assigning a view to the wrong speaker, and they need different
practice.

So: four original items, one from each of four parts, with the transcript, the question, every
option, and the reasoning for all four.

## How to use this page

1. Read the transcript once. Once — the real clips are **played one time only**.
2. Choose your answer before you read a word of the explanation.
3. Read all four explanations, including on items you got right. Right for a shaky reason is a mark
   you lose later.
4. **Note which wrong option you nearly picked, and which trap it was.** That note is the output of
   this page.

One structural point first: **the options are on screen and you read them yourself** — the format
page says "test takers will be required to read answer choices in the Listening Test". That is why
these traps work. A distractor has to survive careful reading, so the ones written are accurate.

## Item 1 — Part 1, Listening to Problem Solving *(original AlmiWorld practice item)*

Part 1 is two speakers working through a problem, in three sections of audio of a minute or so each.

> **Ravi:** The van was supposed to be here Tuesday morning. It's Thursday.
> **Dana:** It left the depot Tuesday at six, but the driver was rerouted to Kingston after a road
> closure.
> **Ravi:** So where is it now?
> **Dana:** Back in our yard, load still on it. I can send it out first thing tomorrow, or you can
> collect it today.
> **Ravi:** I have a crew standing around waiting on that load. Today.

**What does Ravi decide to do?**

- **A. Collect the load from the yard himself, today.** Correct. "Today" is the whole answer — Ravi
  takes Dana's second option without naming it in full.
- **B. Wait for the van to go out tomorrow morning.** Genuinely offered, and offered first. Wrong
  only because he declined it — picking B means you answered from the middle of the exchange.
- **C. Have the load rerouted through Kingston.** The trap. Kingston was said, and is accurate — but
  it answers *why the delivery was late*, not *what Ravi decides*. **An option can be perfectly true
  of the audio and still answer a question nobody asked.**
- **D. Ask for a refund on the delivery charge.** Plausible for an annoyed customer, never said.

## Item 2 — Part 4, Listening to a News Item *(original AlmiWorld practice item)*

Part 4 is one speaker reading a short report, about a minute and a half. News items have sections:
what is happening, why, what it means for you, what is *not* happening. The traps sit in the seams.

> Repairs to the Harbourview pedestrian bridge begin on the twelfth of next month and will last
> eleven weeks. The city says the work was brought forward after an autumn inspection found corrosion
> in two support cables. During the closure, the number 14 bus will run free every fifteen minutes
> across the harbour. A separate proposal to widen the bridge's north approach has been deferred to
> next year's budget. The engineer said the crossing remains safe until the work begins.

**Why is the repair work starting earlier than originally planned?**

- **A. An inspection found corrosion in two of the support cables.** Correct, and signalled by
  "brought forward after".
- **B. The north approach is too narrow for current use.** The trap. Widening the north approach was
  clearly said — but it belongs to the sentence about what has been *deferred*, not to the cause of
  the repairs. **Right report, wrong section of it.** The two sentences are seconds apart, and one
  carries the word "separate" — your warning.
- **C. The bridge has been declared unsafe for pedestrians.** Contradicted at the end. Corrosion
  sounds like danger, so this one is built from your inference, not the report.
- **D. A contractor offered a lower price for winter work.** The kind of thing a city says, and never
  said here.

## Item 3 — Part 5, Listening to a Discussion *(original AlmiWorld practice item)*

Part 5 is three speakers, a video clip, about a minute and a half to two minutes. Three voices means
the test can ask *who* as easily as *what*.

> **Nadia:** Two weeks until intake closes. Where are we on volunteer training?
> **Owen:** Run the whole thing in one Saturday. People forget it if you spread it out.
> **Priya:** One Saturday loses everyone who works weekends. Two evenings, and we record them.
> **Owen:** Record it and half of them won't show up live at all.
> **Nadia:** Then we record, but attendance counts from the live session. Priya, book the room.

**What is Owen's main objection to Priya's plan?**

- **A. Recording the sessions will reduce live attendance.** Correct — his only response to her
  proposal, and the objection Nadia designs around.
- **B. Evening sessions exclude people who work weekends.** The trap. The view is stated accurately
  in the discussion — *by Priya, against Owen*. **The view is real — the name attached to it is not.**
  Attribution traps are the signature of Part 5.
- **C. There is not enough time before intake closes.** Nadia's framing in the first line, not Owen's
  objection.
- **D. The room may not be free for two evenings.** The room appears only in Nadia's instruction, and
  no availability problem is raised.

With three speakers, note initials, not sentences: `O: 1 Sat` / `P: 2 eve + rec` / `O: rec→no show`
answers this item.

## Item 4 — Part 6, Listening to Viewpoints *(original AlmiWorld practice item)*

Part 6 is one speaker for about three minutes — the longest clip in the section. The format page
calls it "Listening to Viewpoints". The study materials say "Listening for Viewpoints". Long
single-voice argument is where concessions get mistaken for conclusions.

> ...I'll grant the critics this much: the four-day week did cost us something. Our response times to
> clients slipped in the first quarter, and two smaller accounts said so plainly. I won't pretend
> that didn't happen. But the slippage sat in the weeks when we were still on the old scheduling
> system, and it flattened out once the rota was rebuilt. Against that, we've had no resignations
> from engineering in fourteen months, in a market where I used to replace two people a year. Would
> I go back? No. The cost was temporary. The gain has held.

**What is the speaker's overall position on the four-day week?**

- **A. It is worth keeping, despite a real cost early on.** Correct. "Would I go back? No" is the
  conclusion, and everything before "But" is ground he gives away first.
- **B. Client response times suffered badly in the first quarter.** The trap, and a true sentence —
  but it is his concession, what he admits in order to argue past it. **Quoting the speaker back
  accurately can still be wrong, because a concession is the part of his own case he has already
  discounted.** Listen for the pivot: *I'll grant*, *I won't pretend*, *against that*, *but*.
- **C. The shorter week cost the company nothing.** The mirror error — deleting the concession rather
  than over-weighting it. He names a cost in his first sentence.
- **D. He would return to five days if resignations rose again.** A condition he never sets.
  Retention is his evidence, not his trigger.

## The four traps, named

- **True but off-question** (Item 1). Accurate detail, answering a question you were not asked.
- **Right report, wrong section** (Item 2). A real detail imported from elsewhere in the passage —
  often from the part about what is *not* happening.
- **Right view, wrong speaker** (Item 3). Part 5's speciality, and why your notes need names.
- **Concession quoted as conclusion** (Item 4). The speaker's admitted cost, sold to you as their
  position.

The honest limitation: **four items is a diagnostic, not a practice volume.** All this page can do is
show you which shape pulls at you.

Recognising a trap once, on the page, with the transcript in front of you, is not the same skill as
beating it under a clock, with the audio playing one time only and the next screen arriving whether
you are ready or not. Reading is the diagnosis. The reflex comes from timed items, done until a pivot
word moves your pen before you have decided anything.
