## What the Reading section actually is

**Three of the four Reading parts are two tasks wearing one number.** Part 1 is an email plus a
response with blanks. Part 2 is a diagram plus an email with blanks. Part 4 is an article plus a
reader's comment with blanks. Only Part 3 is what people picture when they say "reading
comprehension" — one text, one set of questions, done.

That second text is not a footnote. It scores the same as everything else, and it tests something
else entirely.

## The four parts, side by side

| Part | Official name | Qs | Split | The two texts | Register |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reading Correspondence | 11 | 6 + 5 | An email between community members about an everyday social or work situation, then a short response with blanks | "conversational, social, friendly, familiar" |
| 2 | Reading to Apply a Diagram | 8 | 5 + 3 | A diagram with point-form text and visuals, then a short email about the diagram content with blanks | "informative, helpful, not personal" |
| 3 | Reading for Information | 9 | — | A four-paragraph informational text about a person, place, event, or process. No second text | "formal, educational, descriptive" |
| 4 | Reading for Viewpoints | 10 | 5 + 5 | An article with two or more opinions on a community issue, then a short reader's comment with blanks | "formal, complex: similar to a news article" |

Part 3 is also the only part with an option E, for **"information that is not stated in the
reading"**. Do not go hunting for a not-stated escape hatch in Parts 1, 2 or 4 — there isn't one.

## Do the arithmetic

Blanks: 5 in Part 1, 3 in Part 2, 5 in Part 4. That's 13. Questions: 11 + 8 + 9 + 10. The four parts
come to 38, which is what your Reading raw score is reported out of.

**So 13 of 38 marks — a third of the section — are not comprehension questions at all.** They are
"does this sentence still make sense" questions. The comprehension half asks *did you find the
fact*. The blanks half asks *does the sentence hold together*. Different skill, different failure,
different practice. Most study plans treat Reading as one lump and drill only the first kind.

## The failure: grammar-first blank filling

You reach the response email, read the sentence with the gap, open the dropdown, and pick the option
that **sounds right in the sentence**. That instinct is correct in almost every English exam you
have ever taken. It is wrong here.

Call it grammar-first blank filling. The options are usually *all* grammatical — the sentence reads
well with any of them. Only one is consistent with a fact stated in the *other* text, sixty words
away, which you read once and half-forgot.

## Worked example *(original AlmiWorld practice item)*

**Main text — an email from a neighbour, Part 1 style:**

> Plots in the Riverbend community garden are handed out by lottery every March. The one exception
> is volunteers: if you put in four hours or more at any garden work day last season, your plot is
> assigned before the lottery is drawn. You were at the fall clean-up for most of a Saturday, so
> you're in good shape.

**Response text — one sentence, with the blank:**

> Thanks for explaining all this. Since I did six hours at the fall clean-up, I ________ in March.

**The options:**

| | |
|---|---|
| A | will still have to enter the lottery |
| B | won't need to enter the lottery |
| C | am no longer eligible for the lottery |
| D | can enter the lottery twice |

**Answer: B.**

Now the part that matters — why the others fail, given that all four are grammatical and all four
produce a natural-sounding sentence.

**A is a straight contradiction.** Six hours clears the four-hour threshold, so the exception
applies. A is what you choose after skimming "lottery every March" and never reaching the exception.

**C is a meaning reversal dressed in a word from the passage.** "Eligible" *feels* like passage
vocabulary, and being exempt from the lottery and being ineligible for it are both ways of not being
in it. But one is a benefit and one is a bar. C quietly turns the benefit into a penalty. That is
the commonest distractor design in the section: right topic, right register, inverted relationship.

**D invents a rule.** Nothing mentions multiple entries. It is grammatical and it fits the friendly
register perfectly — and it is unsupported. In Parts 1, 2 and 4, unsupported means wrong.

## The shape that works

1. **Read the main text once for structure, not detail.** Who is writing, to whom, and what one
   decision or event sits at the centre of it.
2. **Answer the comprehension questions first.** They send you back into the text, and that second
   pass loads the facts the blanks will need.
3. **Before opening a dropdown, say the fact in your head.** "Volunteers skip the lottery." Now you
   are matching options against a fact, not against a sound.
4. **Treat every option as grammatical.** If two both fit the sentence, you have learned nothing
   yet — go back to the main text.
5. **Watch the relationship words**: still, no longer, instead, unless, except, before, only.
   Reversals live there.
6. **Answer everything.** There are **"no points deducted for wrong answers"**, so a guess is free
   and a blank is not.

## Timing and one-way doors

The section runs **43–56 minutes** — the range exists because the test "may contain unscored content
used for test development". Older PDFs still in circulation quote 55–60 minutes; that figure is
stale.

Reading is timed **per part**, and the test moves on by itself: "the test will automatically move
forward to the next part", and **"you can't go back to a previous part."** Once Part 1 closes, those
11 marks are settled. One caveat — the free official sample test lets you go back and change
answers. The real test does not. Do not build a habit on the sample's behaviour.

## What actually costs you the mark

- Picking the blank that sounds best instead of the one the main text supports.
- Reading the response text on its own, as if it were a standalone grammar exercise.
- Missing an exception clause — *unless*, *except* — in the main text, then contradicting it.
- Choosing a distractor because it reuses a word you recognise from the passage.
- Treating Part 2's diagram as decoration. The diagram *is* the main text.
- Looking for a not-stated option in Parts 1, 2 or 4. Only Part 3 has one.
- Overspending on Part 1 and getting auto-advanced out of a later part.
- Leaving anything blank.
