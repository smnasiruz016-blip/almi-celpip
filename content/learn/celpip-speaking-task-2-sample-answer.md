Most Task 2 answers fail in a way that sounds like success: the speaker narrates competently for
sixty seconds and then stops. Events, in order, ending when the clock ends. **A personal experience
answer needs a point — some reason this memory and not one of the ten thousand others.** The
difference between a story and a sequence of events is that a story arrives somewhere.

So the test of your answer is the last two sentences. If they say what happened next, you told a
sequence. If they say why it mattered, you told a story.

## The task

Task 2 is "Talking about a Personal Experience", the second of the eight Speaking tasks. You get
**30 seconds of preparation and 60 seconds of speaking** — roughly 130 to 160 words at a natural
pace. Those per-task times come from Paragon's Speaking study pack, not from the
[test-format page](https://www.celpip.ca/take-celpip/test-format/), which publishes the eight task
names and the 15-minute component length but not the individual clocks. That is why so few sites
quote them.

Sixty seconds is one small event. Not a year abroad, not a career change. One afternoon.

## The prompt

> Tell me about a time when someone you did not know did something small for you. Describe what
> happened, and explain why you still remember it.
>
> *(original AlmiWorld practice prompt)*

## Thirty seconds of preparation, honestly

You are not planning a structure in thirty seconds. You are making two decisions.

**First: which memory, and does it have a point?** Not "is it interesting" — is there something you
understood afterwards that you did not understand before? If you cannot answer that in the prep
window, pick a different memory. A boring memory with a point beats a good one without.

**Second: what is the one concrete detail that makes it real?** A name, a floor number, a season.
Decide it now, because you will not invent it fluently while speaking.

That is the whole prep. Everything else you can do out loud.

## The answer

> Okay — this one's small, but it's the one I always come back to. My first February in Saskatoon, I
> put a load of laundry in the machine in my building on a Sunday afternoon and then completely
> forgot about it. For about four hours. When I finally went down, someone had already taken
> everything out, folded it, and stacked it on the table, with my one missing sock sitting on top of
> the pile. And honestly, my first feeling wasn't gratitude — it was embarrassment. A stranger had
> folded my socks. It turned out to be Marta from 304, and when I apologised she just said, "People
> needed the machine." That was it. No big deal to her. But I'd been living in that building for five
> months and I hadn't spoken to a single person in it. That's the part that stayed with me — I'd
> decided the place was unfriendly, when really nobody had ever seen me.

*About 155 words. Target: 60 seconds.*

Notice where it ends. The last two sentences are not "and then we became friends" or "and now I
always check my laundry". They are the thing the speaker understood. That is the point arriving.

## Read against the four dimensions

CELPIP publishes four Speaking dimensions: **Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability and Task
Fulfillment** (the
[Performance Standards PDF](https://www.celpip.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/CELPIP-Performance-Standards.pdf),
restated on the test results page). Three of those four are shared with Writing; only the fourth
differs.

One caveat before the reading: **this page does not assign the answer a level, because nobody outside
Paragon can.** There is no answer key for Speaking and no published weighting across the four
dimensions — and even the rater count differs between official pages, the results page saying three
to five speaking raters and the Score Guidance PDF at least three certified raters. What follows is a
reading, not a mark.

**Content/Coherence.** The answer moves on one line — forgot the laundry, found it folded, felt
embarrassed, learned something — with no detour. The turn is signalled out loud: *"And honestly, my
first feeling wasn't gratitude."* A listener never has to hold two threads.

**Vocabulary.** There is no impressive word anywhere in it. What it has instead is precision:
*"stacked it on the table"*, *"my one missing sock sitting on top of the pile"*. Concrete verbs and
nouns do more work here than a rehearsed idiom would.

**Listenability** is officially defined as *"How easy is it to listen to and understand the
response?"*, and its sub-points include *"Rhythm, pronunciation, and intonation"*. The answer is
built out of short sentences and one piece of reported speech — *"People needed the machine"* — which
gives you somewhere natural to change your voice. Worth being exact about this dimension: Paragon
states that *"Our raters specifically do not consider your accent when rating your responses"* and
that accents *"have no bearing on how your CELPIP Speaking component is evaluated"*. But
pronunciation itself is inside the rubric. Accent is not rated; being understood is.

**Task Fulfillment.** The prompt asks for two things — what happened, and why you still remember it.
The answer does both, and the second is not tacked on at the end; it is what the whole thing was
building toward. Beneath this dimension sits the harshest official rule in CELPIP: a response giving
**insufficient information to assess** scores 0 or 1, and those levels map to no CLB level at all.

## What this answer is not

It is not a frame with the nouns swapped out. Three of its sentences work only here: *"A stranger had
folded my socks"* is funny only because it is laundry. *"People needed the machine"* is Marta's line,
not a phrase. *"Nobody had ever seen me"* lands only because five months in one building came before
it.

Memorise those and they will actively cost you on a different prompt, because they will not fit, and
the fit is where the marks are. **This answer works because of its details, and its details are the
part you cannot borrow.**

What you can take is what the answer *does*: it commits in the first sentence, it stays on one
afternoon, and it spends its last two sentences on meaning rather than plot.

## What actually costs you

- Forty of your sixty seconds on setup, then the point announced in a rush at the end.
- A dramatic memory. Drama does the work the telling should do, and hides how much of the mark comes
  from the telling.
- Two stories instead of one, because the first felt too small.
- Ending because the timer ended.
- A polished essay delivered aloud. Real speech restarts, hedges, says "honestly". Yours should.
