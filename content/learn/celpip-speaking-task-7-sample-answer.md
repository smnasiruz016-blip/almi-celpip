One test separates a Task 7 answer with reasons in it from ninety seconds of confident noise.
**Could a reasonable person disagree with this sentence? If not, it is not a reason.** "It would be
better for everyone" fails that test. Every sentence in the answer below survives it.

## The failure

You state your opinion. Then you state it again in warmer words. Then you add an example that turns
out to be the same claim wearing a hat. Ninety seconds gone, one thing said three times, fluently.
**Content/Coherence and Task Fulfillment are two of the four published rating dimensions, and an
answer with one idea in it is thin in both at once.**

The same failure hides inside three reasons that are three angles on one idea: *it saves money, it's
cheaper for the city, taxpayers pay less.* Real reasons sit on different axes — one about cost, one
about fairness, one about what people will actually do.

**And "it depends" is not a position.** It describes the question instead of answering it. A middle
position is fine, but it needs edges — it must rule something out. "I'd support this, but only if X
stays" is a position. "There are good points on both sides" is not.

## The task

Task 7 is **"Expressing Opinions"** — the seventh of the eight Speaking tasks, in a component that
runs **15 minutes**. You get **30 seconds of preparation and 90 seconds of speaking**. Only Task 1
gives you that long — every other task gives 60. Aim for roughly **200–240 words** — an answer that
lands at 130 is half-empty, not crisp. Those clocks come from Paragon's Speaking study pack, not the
test-format page, which publishes the task names but not the timings.

## The prompt

*(original AlmiWorld practice prompt)*

> Your employer is thinking about scrapping the annual performance review and replacing it with a
> short check-in between each employee and their manager once a month. Staff have been asked for
> their opinion. Do you agree with the change? Explain why.

## Thirty seconds of preparation, honestly

You are not outlining an essay. You are settling two things.

First, the position — and if it is conditional, the condition, because that is the edge that makes it
arguable. Here: yes to monthly check-ins, but keep one written record a year. That rules something
out in both directions, which saves it from being "it depends".

Second, two reasons that are not each other. When feedback arrives is one axis — what people will say
out loud is another.

## The answer

> I agree with the change, with one condition I'll come back to at the end.
>
> The main reason is timing. My last review was in March, and half of it was about a report I wrote
> the previous April — eleven months earlier. And she wasn't wrong, actually. The feedback was fair.
> But by March that project was closed, the client had moved on, and there was nothing I could do
> with it. If she'd told me in May, I'd have fixed it in a week.
>
> The second reason is a different thing entirely. It's about what people are willing to say. When
> the one conversation you get all year is also the conversation that decides your raise, nobody
> walks in and admits they're behind on the training, or that they don't understand half of what
> Finance sends them. You go in and you sell yourself. Twenty minutes a month with no money attached
> to it — people will actually put their hand up.
>
> Now, the obvious objection is the paperwork. The annual review leaves a written record, and
> promotions need something on file. That's true, and I wouldn't want to lose it. So keep a one-page
> summary at year end, written up from the twelve check-ins.
>
> Monthly for the real conversation, one page a year for the file. That's what I'd vote for.

Roughly 225 words for 90 seconds — about 150 a minute, unhurried, not a race.

## Read against the four dimensions

**Content/Coherence.** The position lands in the first sentence and is never re-opened. The two
reasons are genuinely separate — one about *when* information arrives, one about *what people will
admit* — and both survive the disagreement test: a manager could argue that March feedback still
helps, or that staff are candid in reviews. The objection is put in its strongest form (*"promotions
need something on file"*), conceded, then answered.

**Vocabulary.** Nothing rare — precision that fits this situation only: *"the client had moved on"*,
*"no money attached to it"*, *"one page a year for the file"*. Concrete words about a real workplace
beat impressive words about nothing.

**Listenability.** Officially, *"How easy is it to listen to and understand the response?"*, with
*"Rhythm, pronunciation, and intonation"* among its sub-points. Read it aloud: short units —
*"And she wasn't wrong, actually."* — and audible signposts, *"The second reason"*, *"Now, the
obvious objection"*. A listener hears the turn coming. Long subordinate chains flatten intonation.

**Task Fulfillment.** The verb was *argue*, and the answer argues: takes a side, supports it, handles
the counter-argument, closes deliberately. Beneath this dimension sits the harshest official rule —
a response giving **insufficient information to assess** scores **0 or 1**, and levels 0 and 1 map to
no CLB level at all.

## What no one can tell you

- **This page does not put a band on that answer, and no page honestly can.** There is no answer key
  for Speaking, no published weighting across the four dimensions, and no rater in the room.
- Paragon's pages disagree even on who marks you — three to five speaking raters on the results page,
  at least three certified raters in the Score Guidance PDF. The FAQ's AI-human hybrid answer names
  **Writing** only — whether Speaking is AI-scored is not published either way.
- What *is* published is the four dimensions. Aim at those.

## What this answer is not

It is not a structure to fill in. Three of its lines exist only because of this prompt: *"My last
review was in March, and half of it was about a report I wrote the previous April"*, *"they don't
understand half of what Finance sends them"*, and *"keep a one-page summary at year end, written up
from the twelve check-ins"*. Take away this review cycle and all three are false.

**This answer works because of its details, and its details are the part you cannot borrow.** Carry
the wording into a prompt about charging for parking downtown and you are reciting furniture from
someone else's house.

What travels is what the answer *does*: commits in the first sentence, puts its reasons on different
axes, makes one concrete, answers the other side, stops on purpose. Habits, not lines.
