## What is actually going wrong

**People who lose marks on CELPIP Reading can nearly always read the passage perfectly well.**

They lose to the clock, to the blanks, or to a distractor built to catch someone who read carefully.
The standard advice — read more English — is the slowest possible route, because it treats a
reading-speed problem, a task problem and a trap problem as one thing.

## The failure: the undiagnosed retake

The four Reading parts come to 38 questions in 43–56 minutes, and a count out of 38 tells you nothing
about which failure took your marks. So someone scores 28, books a retake, studies the same way for
six weeks, and barely moves — the practice was aimed at the average of four separate problems. Below
are the four. Find yours, with your own practice results in front of you.

### 1. Running out of time in one part

**Recognise it:** your wrong answers cluster at the end of a part. Not spread evenly — bunched in the
last two or three items, often with a guess you do not remember making.

**What it is:** the section is timed **per part**, and the test moves on by itself — "the test will
automatically move forward to the next part", and **"you can't go back to a previous part."** So
finishing Part 1 with four minutes to spare does not buy you four minutes in Part 4. Each part is a
closed box.

**The fix is per-part discipline, not faster reading.** Practise one part at a time against a clock,
and treat its last item as a deadline you hit deliberately. Because **there are no points deducted
for wrong answers**, never let a part close with anything unanswered.

One warning: the free official sample test lets you go back and change answers. The real test does
not. If you have only practised there, you may never have felt this failure.

### 2. Losing the blanks

**Recognise it:** you do well on the comprehension questions and badly overall. The gap is the tell.

**What it is:** Parts 1, 2 and 4 each have a second, shorter text with blanks to fill from dropdowns
— 5 in Part 1, 3 in Part 2, 5 in Part 4. That is **13 of the 38 marks on a task that is not
comprehension at all.** That second text is a reply or a comment written by someone who has read the
main text, so a blank tests consistency with the main text, not grammar.

**Every option in a well-built blank is grammatical.** If you choose by ear — which one sounds right
— you are answering a question the test is not asking. Go back to the main text and ask which option
the writer of the reply could actually have written, given what they read.

### 3. Distractors

**Recognise it:** when you check the key, the right answer is obvious the moment you see it. That
reaction means your reading was fine and your *selection* was not.

**What it is:** the wrong options are built from true statements that answer a different question,
details attached to the wrong person, and scope shifts — *some* for *most*, *may* for *must*, *this
year* for *last year*.

**The fix is a checking habit, not more vocabulary.** Before you commit, name what the question asked
about, and confirm the option is about that same one.

### 4. The Part 2 miss

**Recognise it:** Part 2 is the part where your accuracy drops, even though it is the shortest.

**What it is:** in Reading to Apply a Diagram the main text is **"a diagram with point form text and
visuals"** — not prose — and the task is to *apply* it to a person's situation. **So the standard
trap is a true reading of the diagram belonging to the wrong person.** Every distractor can be
verified somewhere in the diagram.

## A worked example

*(original AlmiWorld practice item)*

**Riverbend Community Centre — Winter Registration**

| Stream | Who it is for | Register by | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Waitlist transfer | On last winter's waitlist | 8 Nov | Place automatic, confirm by email |
| Early Bird | Members who joined before 1 September | 15 Nov | Last winter's fee held |
| Household rate | Two or more at one address, at least one a member | 15 Nov | Early Bird stream only |
| Open registration | Anyone, member or not | 1 Dec | Full fee |

> Priya became a member in July. She lives with her brother, who has never joined. Neither was on a
> waitlist last winter. She wants both of them in the program at the household rate.

**What does Priya need to do?**

- **A. Register both of them under Early Bird by 15 November.**
- B. Register both of them by 1 December.
- C. Have her brother become a member first.
- D. Confirm their places by email.

**A is correct.** The household rate is Early Bird only, and Priya joined before the 1 September
cut-off.

**B is true of a different question.** 1 December is a real deadline in the diagram — the open
registration one, at full fee. The date is accurate; it does not go with the rate she wants.

**C is a scope shift.** The diagram says *at least one* a member. C reads that as "each".

**D is a detail from another row.** Confirming by email belongs to waitlist transfer — and neither of
them was on a waitlist. **A true detail attached to the wrong person is the standard Part 2 trap.**

## The shape that works: run a session so it diagnoses

1. Do ten items under a clock, one part at a time. You want detail, not a score.
2. Mark them, but **do not stop at the score.** The score is what hides the diagnosis.
3. For each wrong answer, write which of the four it was: clock, blank, distractor, Part 2.
4. Next to it, write whether you still had time left. Wrong-with-time and wrong-with-none are
   different illnesses.
5. If it was a distractor, add one word: *other question*, *wrong person*, *scope*.
6. **Ten items give you a distribution, and the distribution is your study plan** — four marks in one
   column means you have one problem, not four.

## What actually costs you the mark

- Practising whole sections and only ever recording the total.
- Choosing blanks by what sounds fluent instead of by what the main text established.
- Reading Part 2's diagram thoroughly, then answering about the wrong row.
- Leaving anything unanswered when a part is about to close.
- Rehearsing only where going back is allowed, so you never rehearse the deadline.
- Treating "read more English" as a plan. It is a habit — it is not a diagnosis.
