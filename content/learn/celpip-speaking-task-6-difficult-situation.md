## What Task 6 actually asks

You are placed in an uncomfortable situation. Something has gone wrong, or two people want
incompatible things, and **both available options will disappoint somebody.** You choose one, and you
speak to the person affected.

It is the task people find hardest, and not because of the English.

## The failure: being so polite that nothing is said

The instinct in an awkward moment is to soften. So the answer becomes:

> *"I'm so sorry about this situation. I really appreciate your understanding and I hope we can find
> a solution that works for everyone. I know this is difficult and I feel bad about it. Thank you so
> much for being patient."*

Warm, courteous, and **it does not contain a decision.** The listener still does not know what is
happening. And a marker cannot assess a choice that was never made:

> A response giving **insufficient information to assess** scores **0 or 1**.

**Politeness is the wrapping, not the message.** Wrap something.

## The other failure: choosing without telling them why

The opposite error is blunt:

> *"I've cancelled your booking. Sorry."*

There is a decision, but the person has been given nothing to accept it with. Task 6 is testing
whether you can deliver bad news in a way that survives contact with a real human being.

## The shape that works

1. **Say what has happened**, briefly and plainly. One sentence.
2. **Say what you have decided.** Not what you might do — what you have done or will do.
3. **Give the reason.** Short. People accept a decision far more easily when they understand it.
4. **Offer what you can.** Almost always there is something — a partial fix, an alternative, a
   timeline.
5. **Acknowledge the cost to them, once.** Once is sincere. Four times is padding.

Notice that the apology is **step five, not step one**. Leading with sorry and never arriving at the
decision is the commonest shape of a low answer.

## A worked example

**The prompt** *(original AlmiWorld practice prompt)*

> You manage a small community hall. A family booked it months ago for a wedding this Saturday. A
> pipe has burst and the main room will not be usable until next week. The only other space is the
> smaller side room, which holds forty people; their guest list is ninety. Call the family and tell
> them what you are going to do.

**The response** *(spoken, about 60 seconds)*

> Hello Mr. Farah — I'm calling about Saturday, and I'm afraid I have bad news about the main hall.
>
> A pipe burst on Tuesday night and the room's flooded. It won't be dry and safe until the middle of
> next week, so we cannot use it for the wedding.
>
> Here's what I've done. I've held the side room for you for Saturday at no charge, and I've spoken
> to the church hall on Bellevue — they're free that afternoon and they hold a hundred and twenty.
> If you want it, I'll book it now and we'll pay the difference in the rate.
>
> The side room is yours if you'd rather stay here, but I want to be straight with you: it takes
> forty people, and you have ninety. I don't think it works.
>
> I'm sorry. You booked us in March and this is not the week for it to happen. Tell me which way you
> want to go and I'll make the calls today.

**Why this works.**

- **The bad news arrives in the second sentence**, not the tenth.
- **A decision has already been taken.** He held the side room and rang the church hall before
  calling. That is what a competent person does, and it shows without being announced.
- **The honest sentence is the strongest one.** *"It takes forty people, and you have ninety. I don't
  think it works."* Refusing to pretend the bad option is fine is exactly the judgement being tested.
- **One apology, placed near the end**, where it lands as sincerity rather than as throat-clearing.
- **It closes with a next step.**

## Two things worth knowing

**You may invent everything.** The burst pipe, the church hall, the rate difference — none of it is
in the prompt. Task 6 rewards a person who *solves* the situation, and solving requires details the
prompt never gave you. Make them up.

**"I don't think it works" is not rudeness.** Test-takers avoid direct sentences because they seem
impolite in English. They are not. Clear beats soft, every time, and softness that hides the message
is what actually offends.
