## What Part 2 actually is

In Part 2 there is no passage to read. **The main text is a diagram** — officially, "a diagram with
point form text and visuals". A schedule, a fee table, a grid of options, laid out the way a
community notice board lays things out.

Underneath it sits a second text: "a short email from one community member to another about the
diagram content, with blanks to fill in". Five questions on the diagram, three blanks in the email.
Eight marks. The register is informative and helpful, not personal. That is the whole task — worth
saying plainly, because several pages ranking for this search never say it at all.

Now the part that matters. **The word "Apply" in the official name is the instruction.** You are not
asked what the diagram says. You are asked to take somebody's situation — their age, their address,
their free evening — and work out which row or column applies to *them*.

## The failure: the other person's answer

Here is the shape of almost every Part 2 mistake. You read the option, you check it against the
diagram, it is accurate, you select it. It is wrong.

**In this part the wrong options are usually true readings of the diagram that belong to a different
person.** Right cell, wrong life. They are the answer for somebody who lives in town, or whose child
is older, or who is free on a Wednesday.

Keyword-matching cannot save you, because every option matches a keyword. Something in the diagram —
a footnote, an eligibility line, a bracketed condition — moves the answer one row across.

## A worked item

*(original AlmiWorld practice item)*

**Riverbend Community Centre — Fall Swim Program**

| Program | Days | Time | Ages | Fee (resident) | Fee (non-resident) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preschool Splash | Tue & Thu | 4:30–5:15 p.m. | 3–5 | $60 | $90 |
| Junior Swim I | Mon & Wed | 5:00–6:00 p.m. | 6–10 | $75 | $112 |
| Junior Swim II | Sat | 10:00–11:00 a.m. | 6–10 | $75 | $112 |
| Teen Lifesaving | Fri | 6:30–8:00 p.m. | 11–15 | $95 | $140 |

- Residency is set by the address on your utility bill or library card, not your workplace.
- **Junior Swim I requires completed Preschool Splash or an instructor assessment.** Junior Swim II
  is open to beginners.
- Registration opens 12 August for residents, 19 August for all others.
- 20% household discount from the second child in the same session — weekday programs only.

> **Question.** Bianca lives in Elmsdale township and pays her utilities there, though she works in
> Riverbend and finishes at 5:30 p.m. on weekdays. Her daughter Nadia is seven and has never taken a
> swimming lesson. Which program should Nadia register for, and at what fee?
>
> A. Junior Swim I, $75
> B. Junior Swim I, $112
> C. Junior Swim II, $75
> D. Junior Swim II, $112

**The answer is D.** Every option there is a true cell of that table, and each belongs to a real
person. Only one belongs to Bianca.

- **A — Junior Swim I, $75.** The correct fee, if you live in Riverbend. Bianca only works there,
  and the footnote ties residency to the utility bill, not the workplace. This is a Riverbend
  parent's answer — and the wrong program as well.
- **B — Junior Swim I, $112.** The right non-resident fee, and the natural pick if you stopped at
  "age seven, ages 6–10, first row that fits". But it runs Monday and Wednesday at 5:00 p.m., when
  Bianca is at work, and it asks for Preschool Splash or an assessment, which Nadia does not have.
  This is the answer for a non-resident child with a free weekday and the preschool level done.
- **C — Junior Swim II, $75.** Right program, resident fee. The answer for the Riverbend family
  next to her in the queue.
- **D — Junior Swim II, $112.** Saturday, open to beginners, ages 6–10, non-resident rate. The
  household discount does not touch it: one child, and weekday programs only.

## The email blanks are a different skill

The three blanks are not fact-retrieval. They test whether a sentence stays coherent with what sits
either side of it. Same diagram, Bianca writing to a neighbour:

> "I looked at the Saturday class for Nadia. She has never had a lesson, so the Wednesday group is
> ______ — it asks for a completed preschool level or an assessment first."
>
> A. not an option for her  B. the cheaper of the two  C. open to her as well  D. the one I would
> recommend

**A.** The blank is clamped on both sides: "she has never had a lesson, so" demands a consequence of
being a beginner, and the dash clause after it supplies the reason.

- **B** makes a fee claim the table contradicts — both Junior Swim levels cost the same.
- **C** is fluent English that the next six words flatly reverse.
- **D** fits grammatically, says nothing the diagram supports, and does not follow from "so".

## Read it in the right order

Most people read the diagram first, then the question, then try to hold twelve cells in their head
while they hunt. That is backwards, and it is why the other person's answer looks convincing.

1. **Read the question first and list the constraints.** Non-resident. Age seven. Beginner. Saturday
   only. Four facts, ten seconds.
2. **Read the footnotes and conditions before the grid.** They decide which rows are in play at all.
3. **Now go to the diagram and eliminate rows, not options.** Cross out whatever a constraint kills.
   Usually two rows survive.
4. **Only then look at the options.** With the field down to two rows, a distractor stops sounding
   plausible and starts sounding like somebody else's business.
5. **Ask of each survivor: whose answer is this?** If you can name the person it belongs to, and it
   is not the one in the question, it is a distractor.
6. **Answer everything.** There are no points deducted for wrong answers, and the section is timed
   per part — the test moves forward on its own and you can't go back to a previous part.

## What actually costs you the mark

- Taking the first row where the age or the name matches, before checking the conditions.
- Reading the diagram before you know what you are looking for.
- Missing the footnote that redefines a category — residency, eligibility, prerequisite, cut-off.
- Confusing where someone works with where they live, or what they want with what they qualify for.
- Getting the program right and then reading the fee from the wrong column.
- Treating the three blanks as a fact hunt instead of reading the full sentence around each one.
- Leaving anything blank when the clock runs out. Guess. It costs nothing.
