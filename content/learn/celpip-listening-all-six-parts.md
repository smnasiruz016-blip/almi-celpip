## What the Listening section actually is

Two facts decide this whole section, and they only matter together.

> All audio clips are played one time only.

> Test takers will be required to read answer choices in the Listening Test.

**The audio plays once, and you read the answer options rather than hearing them — so your eyes
have to leave the audio at exactly the moment the audio is still running.** That is the design.
Every one of the six parts is a test of what you can hold in your head while you are looking at
something else.

Most guides file this under "listen carefully". It is not a concentration problem. It is a
scheduling problem: one pass at the sound, printed options competing for the same attention, no
rewind. Paper and a pen are provided at the test centre precisely because the test does not expect
you to hold it all in memory.

The section runs under an hour — about 47–55 minutes (celpip.ca's format page gives 46–55) — with
an on-screen timer, and the test moves you along by itself: "the test will automatically move
forward to the next screen." You cannot return to a previous part once it is behind you.

## All six parts

Thirty-eight questions in total. Clip lengths below come from the official study pack, which says
they are approximate.

| Part | Official name | Qs | Speakers | Clip length | What it's really testing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Listening to Problem Solving | 8 | 2 | 3 sections, each about 1–1.5 min | Whether you can track a situation that keeps changing under you |
| 2 | Listening to a Daily Life Conversation | 5 | 2 | about 1.5–2 min | Attitude and intent — who wants what, and how they feel about it |
| 3 | Listening for Information | 6 | 2 | about 2–2.5 min | Detail retention across the longest two-person stretch |
| 4 | Listening to a News Item | 5 | 1 | about 1.5 min | Structure — one voice, dense facts, no one to interrupt them |
| 5 | Listening to a Discussion | 8 | 3 | **video**, about 1.5–2 min | Keeping three positions apart, with a screen pulling at your eyes |
| 6 | Listening to Viewpoints | 6 | 1 | about 3 min | Stamina, and telling the speaker's view from views they report |

Two things in that table catch people out.

**Part 5 is a video, and it has three speakers.** It is the only video in the section. If your
practice has been audio-only, the surprise on test day is a screen that wants looking at while you
are also meant to be reading options and taking notes — three positions to keep straight, and eight
questions riding on it, tied with Part 1 for the heaviest part.

Part 6 is the longest single clip at about three minutes, and the one where sources disagree on the
name: the format page calls it "Listening to Viewpoints", while the study pack, participant package
and guidebook all call it "Listening **for** Viewpoints". Same part.

Parts 1 to 3 are described as giving you 30 seconds to hear and answer each question. That figure
is stated for those three parts only — do not plan Parts 4, 5 and 6 around it.

## The failure: the eyes-down gap

Here is what actually happens. The audio is playing. You glance down to read the options — several
seconds in which you were not listening. When you look back up, the speakers have moved on, often
past the exact sentence that reverses the answer.

Call it **the eyes-down gap**. It is not that you missed a word. It is that you missed the *update*
— the second half of the conversation where somebody corrects, cancels or complicates the thing
they said thirty seconds earlier. And CELPIP writes distractors out of that first half on purpose.

"Just concentrate" cannot fix a scheduling conflict. Notes can, because a note buys you the right
to look away.

## Worked example

*(original AlmiWorld practice item — Part 1 style)*

> **Woman:** I dropped the bike off Tuesday. They told me Thursday. It's Saturday.
> **Man:** I know, and I'm sorry. The cable came in late from the supplier — but it's here, it
> arrived yesterday morning. The trouble is Devon ordered it, and he's off until Monday.
> **Woman:** So nobody else can put a brake cable on?
> **Man:** Anyone here can fit it. Devon's just the only one who knows which cable he ordered for
> your model.

**Why has the bike not been repaired?**

A. The replacement part has not arrived at the shop.
B. The mechanic who ordered the part is away until Monday.
C. No one else at the shop can fit a brake cable.
D. The woman brought the bike in later than she had planned.

**A and B are the pair that decides this item, and both are supported by something the man
actually said.** A was true — the cable did come in late. Then he updates it: "it's here, it arrived
yesterday morning." A is the right answer to a question about Tuesday and wrong about Saturday. If
your eyes were on the options during that clause, A looks perfect.

B is correct. It survives the update.

C is a half-heard steal from the woman's question, not from the man's answer. He says the opposite:
"Anyone here can fit it." Options built out of the *question* rather than the *reply* are common.

D is plausible-world filler — nothing in the audio says when she meant to come in. Ordinary life
makes it sound reasonable, which is exactly why it is there.

The lesson generalises: **when two options are both true of the clip, one of them is true of an
earlier moment in the clip.** Timing is the distractor.

## The shape that works

1. Read the options **before** the audio starts wherever the screen lets you, and read them for
   shape, not detail — how they differ from each other. Then you can check them by ear.
2. Take notes with your eyes up, not down. Abbreviations, arrows, initials for speakers. You are
   recording the *change*, not the sentence.
3. Write down reversals above all: "but", "actually", "it turns out", "originally". These mark the
   sentence that kills a distractor.
4. Label speakers from the first line — M/W in Parts 1–3, three initials in Part 5. Half of the
   wrong answers are right facts attached to the wrong mouth.
5. When two options both sound right, ask *when* each was true. Pick the one that is true at the end.
6. Answer and move on. The test advances by itself and the previous part is gone — a question you
   are still chewing on is costing you the next clip.

## Where your eyes should be, part by part

- **Parts 1–3 (two speakers):** ears on who is conceding. Notes stay short — positions and reversals.
- **Part 4 (news, one speaker):** no turn-taking to orient you. Numbers, places, names, what changed.
- **Part 5 (video, three speakers):** the screen is a trap for your attention. Give it as little as
  you can — three columns of notes, one per speaker, are worth more than watching.
- **Part 6 (about three minutes, one speaker):** the long one. Track the difference between what the
  speaker thinks and what the speaker says other people think.

## What actually costs you the mark

- Choosing the option that was true earlier in the clip.
- Reading the options while the audio runs, then discovering you missed the correction.
- Attributing a fact to the wrong speaker — the main risk in Part 5, where there are three of them.
- Practising Part 5 with audio only and meeting the video for the first time on test day.
- Transcribing instead of noting. Full sentences take your eyes off the clip and off the options.
- Carrying an unresolved question into the next part, which you cannot go back to anyway.
- Planning Parts 4–6 around the 30-seconds-per-question figure, which is stated for Parts 1–3.
